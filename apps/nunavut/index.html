What is Nunavut Panel?

Nunavut Panel is a free, lightweight daily-information page for Canada's largest and northernmost territory. For ten communities — Iqaluit, Rankin Inlet, Arviat, Baker Lake, Cambridge Bay, Kugluktuk, Igloolik, Pond Inlet, Pangnirtung and Kinngait — it combines an hourly weather feed with computed wind chill, a blizzard caution estimate based on forecast wind and visibility, an aurora outlook built on NOAA's planetary Kp index and adjusted for extreme geomagnetic latitude, a plain-language sealift explainer, a private on-device harvest log, and an Inuktitut syllabics chart with twenty everyday phrases. Everything loads from public APIs (Open-Meteo, NOAA SWPC) with no account, no app install and no tracking, and the page stays usable on the slow or capped satellite connections common across the territory.

Sealift 101 — how Nunavut's annual resupply works

No road or rail reaches any Nunavut community, so almost everything heavy — vehicles, lumber, furniture, appliances, dry food, fuel — arrives once a year by ship. This annual marine resupply is called sealift. Cargo ships load in southern ports such as Montréal, Bécancour or Hay River, sail north after break-up, and anchor off each community, where tugs and barges lighter the cargo ashore across the beach because most communities have no deep-water dock. Residents and businesses book space months ahead, crate their own orders to strict standards, and then wait: a sofa ordered in April may land on the beach in September. Missing the boat means paying airfreight rates — often several dollars per kilogram — or waiting a full year for the next sailing.

Sealift deadlines, sailing windows and carriers

The shipping season is short because sea ice controls everything. First sailings into Hudson Bay and the eastern Arctic typically depart late June to July; the last ships for high-Arctic communities such as Pond Inlet sail in September and the season closes in October. Booking and cargo-delivery deadlines fall in spring, usually April to June depending on carrier, destination and sailing number. Typical structure:

The main eastern-Arctic carriers are NEAS (Nunavut Eastern Arctic Shipping) and NSSI (Nunavut Sealink & Supply, Desgagnés group); Kitikmeot communities are also served via Hay River on the Mackenzie system. Always confirm the current year's schedule and deadlines on the carrier's own site.

Why a couch costs what it costs in Nunavut

Southern Canadians are often shocked by Arctic prices, but the arithmetic is straightforward. A couch bought in Ottawa must be trucked to a port, professionally crated, booked onto one of a handful of annual sailings, carried two thousand kilometres or more through ice-prone waters, transferred ship-to-barge-to-beach without a crane dock, and stored against weather at both ends. Sealift is the cheap option — rates are quoted per cubic metre or per tonne and are far below airfreight — yet still adds hundreds of dollars to bulky items. Perishables can't wait for the boat at all: they fly, subsidized in part by the federal Nutrition North program for eligible foods. That is why milk is flown, flour comes by ship once a year, and "Amazon Prime" in practice means slow, weight-limited parcel post to most communities — with Iqaluit the frequent exception.

Hunting management: HTOs, quotas and tags

Harvesting in Nunavut is co-managed under the Nunavut Agreement. The Nunavut Wildlife Management Board (NWMB) makes decisions on total allowable harvest; the Government of Nunavut's Department of Environment administers regulations; and each community's Hunters and Trappers Organization (HTO) allocates community quotas, issues tags for species under limitation — polar bear is the best-known example — and sets local rules. Inuit harvesting rights are constitutionally protected, while non-beneficiaries need licences and, for big game, often a licensed outfitter and guide. Some caribou herds are under strict total allowable harvest or moratorium (Baffin Island caribou, for instance), while ringed seal and Arctic char remain staple, largely unrestricted country foods for Inuit. The practical rule for anyone: talk to the local HTO first — quotas and seasons change and the community office has the current answer.

Wind chill and blizzard safety in Nunavut

Nunavut cold is a wind problem as much as a temperature problem. Environment and Climate Change Canada's wind chill index expresses how fast exposed skin loses heat; at typical winter values frostbite risk rises sharply. A blizzard in Canadian warning criteria means winds of 40 km/h or more with visibility reduced to 400 m or less by snow or blowing snow, sustained for hours — in the North the required duration is longer, and ground blizzards can occur under clear skies. Common planning thresholds:

−25 to −39Frostbite possible on exposed skin in 10–30 minCover skin; plan short exposures
−40 to −47Frostbite in 5–10 minLimit outdoor time; travel with gear
−48 to −54Frostbite in 2–5 minAvoid travel; emergency plan essential
−55 and colderFrostbite in under 2 minStay indoors

Aurora at high latitude — why Kp matters less here

Aurora forecasts built for southern Canada obsess over the Kp index: the higher the global geomagnetic activity, the farther south the auroral oval reaches. Nunavut flips that logic. Communities like Rankin Inlet, Arviat, Baker Lake and Iqaluit sit under the auroral oval, so displays are common on any dark, clear night even at Kp 0–2. Farther north it inverts again: Cambridge Bay, Kugluktuk and especially Pond Inlet lie at such high geomagnetic latitude that during quiet conditions the oval is often south of the observer — look toward the southern horizon — and a major storm can push the action away rather than toward you. The real limiting factors in Nunavut are darkness (no aurora viewing under the midnight sun) and cloud, which is why this panel pairs NOAA's live Kp with local cloud cover instead of treating Kp as the whole story.

The Inuktitut writing system, explained

Inuktitut in most of Nunavut is written in qaniujaaqpait — syllabics adapted in the 19th century from the Cree syllabary and later standardized. Each character encodes a consonant-plus-vowel syllable, and the same shape rotates or flips to change its vowel: ᐱ pi, ᐳ pu, ᐸ pa. Long vowels take a dot above (ᐲ pii), and small raised finals write a syllable-closing consonant, as in ᓄᓇᕗᑦ nu-na-vu-t. Western communities such as Kugluktuk and Cambridge Bay traditionally use Inuinnaqtun, written in Latin letters, and dialects differ noticeably from Kivalliq to North Baffin. Inuktut (the umbrella term) is an official language of Nunavut alongside English and French. The panel's language tab includes the core syllabics grid and twenty starter phrases — enough to read community signs and greet people politely.

Flying in Nunavut — weather delays are normal

Every Nunavut community depends on scheduled air service, and Canadian North operates the backbone network, with Calm Air serving the Kivalliq region. What surprises visitors is how routinely weather reshuffles schedules: blowing snow can drop visibility below landing minima in minutes, and a mechanical delay in Iqaluit ripples down the line for days. Experienced travellers build slack into connections, keep medications and a day's essentials in carry-on, and treat the airline's own status page as the source of truth. This panel estimates weather-delay risk from forecast gusts, visibility and snowfall at the selected community — a planning hint, never a substitute for the airline's operational decisions.

Frequently asked questions

What is sealift in Nunavut?
Sealift is the once-a-year marine resupply of Nunavut communities. Cargo ships from southern ports deliver vehicles, building materials, furniture and dry goods between roughly July and October, landing cargo by barge because most communities lack a deep-water dock. Orders must usually be booked and delivered to the port in spring.

When are sealift order deadlines?
Typically April to June, varying by carrier (NEAS, NSSI), destination and sailing number. First-sailing deadlines come earliest. Check the carrier's published schedule for the current year — miss it and you wait for the next sailing or pay airfreight.

What Kp index do I need to see aurora in Nunavut?
Usually very little. Central Nunavut sits under the auroral oval, so Kp 0–2 with a dark, clear sky is often enough. In the far north (Pond Inlet, Cambridge Bay) look toward the south during quiet conditions, and remember there is no aurora viewing during the midnight-sun weeks.

How is wind chill calculated?
This panel uses the Environment Canada / JAG-TI formula from air temperature and wind speed, valid for temperatures at or below about 10 °C and wind of at least 5 km/h. At −30 °C with a 30 km/h wind the wind chill is near −46 — frostbite territory within minutes.

Can I hunt in Nunavut as a visitor?
Non-beneficiaries need territorial licences, and big game (polar bear, muskox, caribou where permitted) generally requires tags allocated through a community HTO and a licensed outfitter. Rules change by species, zone and year — contact the Government of Nunavut Department of Environment and the local HTO before planning anything.

Is my harvest log uploaded anywhere?
No. The log is stored only in your browser's localStorage on your device. Clearing browser data deletes it, and it never substitutes for any official reporting an HTO or the GN may require.
