What is Pinas Panel?

Pinas Panel is a free, data-light utility page for daily life in the Philippines. It puts four essentials on one fast page: typhoon readiness — plain-language action guides for PAGASA Tropical Cyclone Wind Signals #1 to #5, live wind and rain numbers for Manila, Cebu, Davao and Tacloban, and recent magnitude-4.5+ earthquakes near the archipelago from the USGS feed; money tools — live mid-market USD/PHP and other major rates, a currency converter, and an OFW remittance true-cost calculator that exposes the hidden exchange-rate margin on top of the transfer fee; government status portals — SSS, PhilHealth, Pag-IBIG, PSA civil registry, DFA passport appointments, LTO licensing and the COMELEC precinct finder, each with a short how-to; and power outage help — Meralco advisories, a brownout explainer and an ERC complaint link. No app install, no account, and it loads fine on prepaid data.

PAGASA Wind Signal #1–#5: what to actually do

PAGASA raises Tropical Cyclone Wind Signals over specific provinces, and each number is a forecast of wind strength plus a lead time — the higher the signal, the stronger the coming wind and the less time you have. The mistake many households make is treating Signal #1 and #2 as "nothing days." They are your preparation window: once Signal #3 winds arrive, hardware stores are shut, queues at ATMs are long, and going outside to fix a roof is dangerous. Use the table below as a minimum action list, and always follow your barangay and LGU evacuation orders — storm surge and landslides kill more people in the Philippines than wind does.

#262–88~24 hStock 3 days of water and food, buy prepaid load, tie down light roofing, avoid small-boat sea travel, withdraw some cash.
#389–117~18 hStay indoors, expect brownouts and water interruption, evacuate immediately if your LGU orders it or you live in a surge or landslide zone.

One more habit worth building: when a signal is raised, note which signal number your province is under, not just the storm's name. The same typhoon can put one province under Signal #4 and its neighbour under #1, and your action list depends on your own number, not the headline.

Your 72-hour go-bag: the typhoon kit that actually gets used

A go-bag is only useful if it is packed before Signal #2. Build it around 72 hours of self-sufficiency per person: at least three litres of drinking water per day, ready-to-eat food (canned goods with a manual opener, biscuits), any maintenance medicines in a waterproof pouch, a flashlight with spare batteries, a power bank charged to full, a whistle, and copies of key documents — PSA birth certificates, IDs, land titles, insurance — sealed in a zip bag or saved as photos on your phone. Add cash in small bills, because GCash and card terminals die with the signal towers. Families with infants or seniors should pack for their needs first; those items disappear from sari-sari stores fastest. Refresh the food and batteries every six months — set a phone reminder on New Year and on the start of habagat season.

OFW remittances: the fee is not the real cost

The Philippines receives tens of billions of US dollars in remittances every year, mostly through a handful of corridors — the United States, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Singapore, Japan and Hong Kong. Most senders compare providers by the advertised fee, but the bigger cost usually hides in the exchange rate. A provider that charges "zero fees" but converts your dollars at 2–3% below the mid-market rate quietly takes more than one charging a flat $4.99 at a fair rate. On a $500 transfer, a 2.5% FX margin is $12.50 — invisible on the receipt, very visible in the pesos your family receives. The calculator in the PERA tab does the honest math: enter the amount, the fee and the rate you were quoted, and it compares the result against today's mid-market rate to show your true total cost as a percentage. Below roughly 3–4% total is a competitive deal for the US–PH corridor.

How to check your SSS contributions and status online

The Social Security System moved almost everything to the My.SSS portal, and checking your posted contributions no longer requires a branch visit. First, register a My.SSS account at sss.gov.ph — you will need your SSS number, a valid email, and one detail from your records (such as an employer ID or a previously paid receipt) to prove identity. Second, once the confirmation email arrives, log in and open Inquiry → Contributions to see every posted month; gaps usually mean an employer remitted late or not at all, which you can dispute. Third, for loans, go to Inquiry → Loan Status to track applications and balances. The mobile app mirrors the portal. Checking is free — any site or "fixer" charging for an SSS status check is a scam.

How to check PhilHealth membership and contributions

PhilHealth's Member Portal lets you confirm that your premiums are actually posted — worth doing before any planned hospitalisation. Step one: create an account through the official PhilHealth site at philhealth.gov.ph (follow the Member Portal link); you need your PhilHealth Identification Number (PIN) and the email registered to your record. Step two: after activating via the emailed link, log in and open the contributions view to see posted premiums by period — employer-remitted months should appear a month or two after payroll deduction. Step three: if months are missing, gather your payslips and raise it with your HR or the nearest PhilHealth office. Keep your PIN somewhere safe: hospitals ask for it at admission, and retrieving a forgotten PIN takes longer than the check itself.

Brownout vs blackout — and why yours is "rotational"

Filipinos say brownout for almost any outage, but the terms differ. A true brownout is a deliberate voltage reduction — lights dim, fans slow — used to shave demand without cutting anyone off. A blackout is a total loss of supply, whether from a downed line, a tripped plant or a storm. A rotational brownout (technically rotational load dropping) happens when the grid operator orders distribution utilities to shed load because available generation cannot meet demand — typically on hot dry-season afternoons when plants are on outage. Your feeder gets switched off for a scheduled window, then the next feeder takes its turn. That is why advisories list feeders and time slots rather than whole cities. During any outage: unplug sensitive appliances, keep the fridge closed, and report downed lines — never touch them — to your utility's hotline.

Earthquakes in the Philippines: reading the feeds calmly

The Philippines sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire, so magnitude-4 to 5 earthquakes near the trench systems are routine background activity, not a reason to panic. The BAGYO tab lists recent magnitude-4.5+ events near the archipelago straight from the USGS public feed; for official local intensity reports and tsunami advisories, PHIVOLCS is the authority. Two numbers matter when you read a quake entry: magnitude (energy at the source) and depth — a shallow magnitude-5.5 under a city shakes far harder than a deep magnitude-6.5 offshore. If you feel strong shaking: drop, cover and hold on; do not run outside during the shaking; and if you are on the coast and the shaking is long or violent, move to high ground without waiting for a formal tsunami warning.

Official portals worth bookmarking (PSA, DFA, Pag-IBIG, LTO, COMELEC)

A surprising amount of Philippine paperwork is now genuinely online — if you know the real site. Order PSA birth, marriage and CENOMAR certificates for delivery via PSAHelpline.ph or PSA Serbilis. Book passport appointments only at passport.gov.ph — the DFA slots are free to book; anyone selling slots is a fixer. Pag-IBIG savings, loans and MP2 live in Virtual Pag-IBIG. Driver's licence renewals and student permits run through the LTO's LTMS portal. Before an election, confirm your registration and precinct with the COMELEC voter verifier. All of these are free or fixed-fee — treat any site asking for "processing fees" outside these domains as fraudulent.

Frequently asked questions

Is Pinas Panel free?

Yes. It is a static page with no ads, no account, no paywall and no install. Your settings (language, chosen city, last tab) are stored only in your own browser via localStorage.

Where does the typhoon and wind signal information come from?

The Wind Signal explainers summarise PAGASA's public Tropical Cyclone Wind Signal system. Live wind and rain numbers come from the free Open-Meteo API, and earthquake entries come from the public USGS GeoJSON feed. For warnings that affect your safety, always defer to PAGASA, PHIVOLCS and your LGU.

Are the exchange rates real-time?

Rates come from open.er-api.com, which publishes mid-market reference rates updated about once a day. They are for comparison, not execution — banks and remittance providers apply their own rates and margins.

Do the USSD codes work on all networks?

Each code is telco-specific: *143# is Globe, *123# serves Smart and TNT, and DITO is primarily app-based. Codes change over time, so verify with your telco's official site or app before relying on one.

Does this site store my personal data?

No. There are no accounts and no server-side storage. The page never asks for your SSS, PhilHealth or any ID numbers — status checks happen on the official government portals it links to, not here.

What is the difference between a brownout and a blackout?

A brownout is a deliberate voltage reduction (lights dim but stay on); a blackout is a total loss of power. What most Filipinos call a rotational brownout is scheduled load dropping — feeders are switched off in turns when generation cannot meet demand.
