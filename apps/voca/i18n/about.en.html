About the operator · Why I built it

Last updated: 2026-07-16 · Operated by Y-Systems

VOCA DECK started from one question: “What happens if flipping through a vocabulary deck takes zero friction?” This page answers who built it, where your data goes, and how this site makes money.

This tool and its articles grew out of watching up close how vocabulary memorization actually works in the classroom, and how it falls apart. Still, the articles are not built on anecdotes alone. They are checked against what published learning-science research (the forgetting curve, the spacing effect, retrieval practice) can actually support, and anything drawn from classroom experience is labeled as experience. Claims without evidence are marked as having no evidence, and the areas this tool does not cover are collected separately in the limitations section. One person handles design, development, writing and operations, so responsibility for any mistake is clear — it is all mine.

Why I built it

A paper vocabulary book makes you flip pages, cover answers with your hand, and remember where you left off. These small frictions add up until they eventually make you “just not do it.”

Memory is ultimately a function of how many times you encounter something. Meeting a word 100 times with a frictionless method often beats meeting it 10 times with a perfect one. So I built a deck that advances on its own once you press a single play button.

Credit for the original — AnyMemory

I did not invent this “Flashing Memorizer” method myself. The original is AnyMemory, a freeware vocabulary memorization program distributed in South Korea back in the Windows XP era. VOCA DECK was rebuilt with that program as its reference — the approach of showing a word and its meaning alternately in large type — so that it runs in the browser with no installation, together with CSV decks, memorized marks and TTS.

AnyMemory was distributed free of charge at the time through download archives such as that of BetaNews (a Korean tech news site), but the original files have since been taken down, and I could not find the author’s name in any public source. If you know who made it, please let me know and I will credit the original properly here. My thanks to the original author for leaving us such a good method.

That said, I am honest about this method’s limits too. Just watching passively creates an “illusion of knowing.” That is why the How it works article has a separate section titled “Flashing alone is not enough” explaining how to work retrieval practice in. The goal is not to sell a tool but for you to actually remember the words.

Where does your data go?

Your study data goes nowhere. It is never sent to a server.
  • There is no sign-up. No email, no password.
  • The decks (CSV) you open and your memorized marks are stored only in your browser’s localStorage.
  • Uploaded CSV files are not sent to a server. They are read only inside your browser.
  • Your data does not follow you when you switch devices. Keep your original CSV files somewhere safe.

Concretely, it works like this. When you open a CSV, the browser’s FileReader reads the file on your own device, converts it into cards, and saves the result (decks, memorized marks, playback settings, language choice) to localStorage entries named voca:*. Decks saved to “My Voca Decks” live in the same place. Pressing Export backup simply downloads this data to your device as a JSON file; again, nothing goes to a server. TTS also uses the browser’s built-in speech synthesis (speechSynthesis), so your words are not sent to any external service.

I am honest about the cost of this design, too. Since there is no account on a server, clearing your browser data or using a private window means your study history is gone, with no way to recover it. There is no syncing between devices either. That is why I recommend keeping your original CSV files and exporting backups regularly. It is the result of choosing not to collect data over convenience (syncing).

The one exception — if you attach a screenshot when writing in through the contact page, that image is uploaded to our server storage so it can be included in the email as a link, and it is automatically deleted after 90 days. The study app itself never uses this path. Details are in the Privacy Policy.

Editorial principles

All of this site’s explanatory articles (How it works, Study strategy, CSV guide, Spaced repetition compared, and others) are written according to the principles below.

  1. Limits come first. No article is published without stating the cases where our method does not work. In fact, the limitations section of How it works opens with “Flashing alone is not enough.”
  2. The learning-science basis is stated. Not “it works” but “why it works.” The articles rely on well-established concepts such as the forgetting curve, the spacing effect and retrieval practice, and weak evidence is flagged as weak.
  3. Competing tools are compared honestly. We never claim only ours is good. The articles actually say that Anki-style tools are better if you need long-term review scheduling.
  4. No exaggeration. No promises like “3,000 words in a month.” How much you memorize is decided not by the tool but by the number of encounters and by retrieval practice.
  5. Corrections are recorded. Any correction that affects content is noted on the page along with its date, and every content page shows a “Last updated” date at the top.

What this site does not do

  • ❌ Guarantee grades or exam results — the tool only reduces the friction of repetition; in the end, you are the one who memorizes.
  • ❌ Require sign-up — every feature works without an account.
  • ❌ Collect study data — we cannot know what you are memorizing, and we do not need to.
  • ❌ Sell or partner on textbooks and courses — since nothing is sold, there is no reason for the articles to be bent toward selling.
