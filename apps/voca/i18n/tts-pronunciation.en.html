Learn pronunciation with TTS

Last updated: 2026-07-14 · By Y-Systems

“Turning on TTS helps” is only half true. That's because what you gain from audio is completely different from language to language. If you learn Chinese without TTS, half of every word never makes it into memory at all. With Spanish, on the other hand, you can reconstruct the pronunciation almost perfectly with TTS turned off. This article breaks down that gap language by language, shows how to write your CSV for each one, and works out how many seconds the playback gap needs for you to have time to repeat — all based on the actual rules and numbers in the app's code.

The bottom line first — how much each language depends on TTS

This is both the summary and the core of the article. It's ranked by one question: “What do you lose if you turn TTS off?” The more stars, the more hard-to-repair damage you take by memorizing with your eyes alone.

This ranking is based on a single criterion: “how much does the spelling tell you about the pronunciation?” For listening practice and getting used to speed, TTS is useful in every language. Spanish's single star doesn't mean “TTS is useless” — it means “you need it less for figuring out pronunciation.”

Why the gap exists (briefly)

In How it works, there's a passage that says “turning on TTS adds an auditory pathway” and moves on after a single sentence. This article picks up from there. I won't repeat the dual-coding story, though. What matters more than gaining one extra retrieval cue is this.

When you learn a word with your eyes only, your head also stores the sound you made up when reading it. It's not that you store no sound. You store the wrong sound. For someone who memorized colonel as “KOL-oh-nel,” the real colonel (“KER-nul”) spoken by a native speaker is not a word they know, but a word they're hearing for the first time. They may know the meaning perfectly, yet their listening score is zero.

And unlearning this error costs more than learning it right. Correcting a pronunciation that has already settled in takes far longer than putting in a new one from a blank slate. That's why TTS isn't a feature to switch on later — it's one to switch on from the very start.

In one line — the essence of TTS isn't “adding an auditory cue” but “blocking wrong pronunciations from being stored in the first place.” So the more a language's spelling betrays its pronunciation, the bigger the effect. The order in the table above is exactly that order.

Prescriptions by language

Chinese — the one case where the written form doesn't tell you the actual sound

Chinese gets “★★★★★” not just because of tones. Tones are marked in pinyin, so if that were all, you should be able to learn them by eye. The problem is that the tone written in pinyin and the tone actually pronounced are different.

First, let's see that tone is the meaning itself. It's the same ma every time, yet each is a different word.

TonePinyinCharacterMeaning
1st tone (high and level)mā妈mom
2nd tone (rising)má麻hemp / numb
3rd tone (dips, then rises)mǎ马horse
4th tone (falling)mà骂to scold
Neutral tonema吗question particle

Here's what happens in real life when you miss a tone.

  • 我想问你 wǒ xiǎng wèn nǐ — “I'd like to ask you”
    我想吻你 wǒ xiǎng wěn nǐ — “I'd like to kiss you” (just one change, 4th tone → 3rd tone)
  • 买 mǎi to buy ↔ 卖 mài to sell — opposite meanings split by nothing but 3rd vs. 4th tone.
  • 睡觉 shuìjiào to sleep ↔ 水饺 shuǐjiǎo boiled dumplings — slur the tones and “I'm going to sleep” becomes “I'm going to dumpling.”

So far, a careful look at the pinyin solves everything. The real problem starts here.

Tone sandhi (變調) — read it as written in pinyin and you'll be wrong
3rd + 3rd → 2nd + 3rd. 你好 is written in pinyin as nǐ hǎo (3rd + 3rd), but it's actually pronounced ní hǎo (2nd + 3rd).
一: originally 1st tone yī, but it changes to 一个 = yí ge and 一起 = yì qǐ.
不: originally 4th tone bù, but it changes to 不是 = bú shì.
The trouble is that the notation isn't consistent. Third-tone sandhi (你好) is never reflected in pinyin — it's always written nǐ hǎo and read ní hǎo. 一/不 sandhi, by contrast, varies by textbook — some write the changed form (yí ge, bú shì), others the original tone (yī ge, bù shì). In other words, you can't trust the notation. In the end, there is no reliable method other than checking by ear.

This is why a Chinese word learned by eye alone is only “half a word.” There's an extra layer the notation doesn't tell you, and that layer exists only as sound. It's even worse for speakers of a non-tonal language such as English. Because their native language has no tones, they process tone not as a phoneme that changes meaning but as mere tone of voice or intonation. Unless you deliberately feed in the sound, tones don't even get encoded into memory.

The single most important setting for Chinese learners — in Advanced settings, choose the voice yourself. Low-quality local Chinese voices smear the tone contours. In other languages that's merely “a bit robotic,” but in Chinese the very information you need to learn disappears. Don't leave it on the default “Auto.” The detailed reasons are in the limits section below.

English — a language with more exceptions than rules

English is a language that almost seems designed so that guessing pronunciation from spelling gets it wrong. Historically, it borrowed words from many languages; the spelling kept the original while only the pronunciation changed. French — you can read it, but you can't reverse it

French is often misunderstood. The claim that “French spelling and pronunciation don't match” isn't accurate. French is fairly regular in the spelling → sound direction. Learn the rules and you can read words you've never seen. The problem is that the sound → spelling direction is nearly impossible, and that those rules run exactly against most learners' intuition.

Let's look at the most striking examples.

WordLettersActual soundWhat's left
eaux (waters, plural)4/o/One sound — four letters make a single “o”
oiseau (bird)6/wazo/No letter maps one-to-one to a sound
beaucoup (a lot)8/boku/Final p is silent
temps (time)5/tɑ̃/m, p and s all vanish, leaving only a nasal vowel
femme (woman)5/fam/It's an e, but sounds like “a”
monsieur8/məsjø/Almost unrelated to the spelling

Final consonants are mostly silent (c, r, f and l are frequent exceptions that survive — remember them with the English word “CaReFuL”). And nasal vowels (/ɑ̃/ /ɛ̃/ /ɔ̃/) are sounds that don't exist in English. A sound that doesn't exist in your language in principle cannot be learned by eye. This isn't a matter of effort.

The real trap in French is liaison, and TTS won't catch it either.
Learn les (“lay”) and amis (“ah-mee”) separately and you get “lay ah-mee.” In reality it's /le.za.mi/ “lay-zah-mee”. The silent s comes back to life when it meets the vowel of the next word and is pronounced /z/.
The problem is — a TTS that reads one word at a time can never show you liaison. les on its own will forever be “lay.”
Prescription: in French, make cards out of “phrases,” not words. Put 2–3 words on one card, like les amis, vous avez or petit ami, and only then will TTS let you hear the liaison.

Japanese — kanji don't tell you how to read them

In Japanese, anything written in kana is almost perfectly regular. In that respect it's on par with Spanish. But the moment kanji are mixed in, predictability converges to zero.

It's common for a single kanji to have more than ten readings, counting on'yomi and kun'yomi together. 生 alone can be せい, しょう, なま, き, いきる, うまれる, はえる, おう…

  • 生憎 = あいにく (unfortunately) — neither of the two kanji gives any hint of this reading. That's because it's an ateji (当て字), where the kanji were attached to the sound afterwards.
  • 一日 is both ついたち (the 1st of the month) and いちにち (one day). Same spelling, different reading, different meaning.
  • 明日 = あした / あす / みょうにち — which one depends on context and formality.

On top of that comes pitch accent. 箸 (chopsticks) and 橋 (bridge) are both written identically as はし, but their pitch patterns differ. They can't be told apart in writing — only by ear. (Speakers of languages without pitch accent, such as English or Korean, often can't perceive this difference at all.)

Good news for Japanese learners — our app's parenthesis rule was built for exactly this problem. Write 生憎（あいにく） on side A and the screen shows both the kanji and the reading, while the voice reads only あいにく. The very next section covers this rule in detail.

Russian — if you don't know the stress, you don't even know the vowel

Russian has two betrayals stacked on top of each other. I'll keep it short.

  1. Stress position isn't written. Stress marks appear only in textbooks and dictionaries; real Russian texts don't use them. The position is irregular, so you simply have to memorize it.
  2. Stress changes the sound of the vowels (akanye). An unstressed о shifts toward “a” rather than “o”. молоко (milk) would be “mo-lo-ko” as spelled, but it's actually /məlɐˈko/ “muh-lah-KOH”. Without knowing the stress, you can't even tell which vowels survive.

What's more, stress position splits words apart. за́мок (castle) and замо́к (lock) are spelled exactly the same. Given a single word, TTS doesn't know which one either — it's the same problem as English heteronyms, and the fix is the same too (a card with context).

Spanish — why you can turn TTS off

To be honest: for Spanish learners, TTS is barely needed as a “tool for figuring out pronunciation.” That's because in Spanish, the spelling essentially fully determines the pronunciation. Know just the three stress rules below, and you can read even unfamiliar words correctly.

RuleStress positionExamples
Ends in a vowel, n or sSecond-to-last syllableca-sa → CA-sa · co-men → CO-men
Ends in any other consonantLast syllableha-blar → ha-BLAR · fe-liz → fe-LIZ
Exceptions to the two rules aboveAlways marked with an accentca-fé · lá-piz · te-lé-fo-no

Add a few letter rules (h is always silent, ll and ñ, v and b are practically the same sound, c+e/i and z are /θ/ or /s/ depending on region) and that's it. If you go looking for exceptions, you'll find little beyond loanwords.

So Spanish learners should use TTS for something else. Not for figuring out pronunciation, but for getting used to speed. The real hurdle in Spanish isn't “reading” but vowel linking (synalepha) and speaking speed. Vowels between words blur together, and the pace is fast. Raise the reading speed to 1.3–1.5× and run through your cards. It's not pronunciation study, it's listening training. You're using the same TTS feature for a completely different purpose.

What the app actually does — rules set by the code

From here on, this isn't general theory but behavior actually hard-coded into VOCA DECK. Knowing it lets you build much better CSVs.

Rule 1 — if there are parentheses, side A reads “only what's inside”

The app's speech function looks for parentheses first in the card text. It recognizes both full-width （） and half-width ().

  • If side A (the word) has parentheses → it reads only what's inside them. Anything outside is not read at all.
  • Side B (the meaning) → it deletes the parentheses and their contents entirely and reads only the rest.
  • The screen always shows the full original text as-is. Only the sound is removed.

This asymmetry is the key. It means you can design “what you see” and “what you hear” separately. 

生憎（あいにく）,unfortunately
  → Screen: 生憎（あいにく）   (both kanji and reading shown)
  → Sound:  あいにく            (only the reading is heard)

买,buy（mǎi）
  → Screen: buy（mǎi）         (pinyin visible on screen)
  → Sound:  "buy"              (pinyin not read — side B removes parentheses)

Note the second example. Put the pinyin in side B's parentheses and you get a state where you can check the pinyin with your eyes, but the pinyin never intrudes on what you hear. For Chinese learners, this is the best arrangement.

Rule 2 — each side has its own language (voice)

Sides A and B are read with different speech locales. The defaults are A = Japanese (ja-JP), B = Korean (ko-KR), and in Advanced settings you can choose from 13 locales (en-US · ja-JP · ko-KR · zh-CN · zh-TW · th-TH · es-ES · fr-FR · de-DE · it-IT · pt-PT · ru-RU · nl-NL).

This is the No. 1 cause of “there's no sound.” If you load an English deck but side A's locale is left at the default, Japanese (ja-JP), a Japanese voice tries to read English words. It sounds strange, or produces no sound at all.
If you made the CSV yourself, be sure to set side A's language in Advanced settings first. (Applying a sample deck sets the locale automatically.)

Rule 3 — reading speed is limited to 0.5–2.0×

You adjust sides A and B independently, from 0.5× to 2.0×, in steps of 0.05. Values outside that range are forcibly clamped by the code.

And here there's a limit I have to state honestly. The browser's speed control works by stretching the time while keeping the pitch. So below 0.7× you don't get a “slow native speaker,” you get a “weird sound.” More importantly, slowing it down removes the reductions and linking that are the heart of real pronunciation. A water heard at 0.6× is not the water you'll hear in real life. Slow listening is for checking tones and phonemes, not for real listening practice.

Rule 4 — repeat playback: 1–20 times, or infinite

You can have the same audio repeat up to 20 times on one card, or set it to repeat infinitely. Infinite repeat reads it through once, pauses 0.25 seconds, then starts again. Moving to the next card or turning TTS off stops it immediately.

Use infinite repeat when you're drilling a single tone into your body, or hammering away at one sound you can't get right. For regular study, 1–2 times is enough.

Rule 5 — volume is multiplied (a trap)

The actual volume is calculated like this. 

Actual volume = (Master volume ÷ 100) × (that side's volume ÷ 100)

Master 50% + Side A 50%  →  0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25  →  actually 25%

Turn both sliders down and they multiply, ending up far quieter than you'd expect. If “it's too quiet,” check here first. Conversely, it's useful to set only side A loud and side B quiet (hear the foreign language loudly, and the meaning in your own language like background audio).

Rule 6 — you can choose what gets read

Choose from side A only / side B only / both. For most learners, “side A only” is the right choice. There's rarely any reason to hear the meaning in your own language as audio, and a native-language voice cutting in breaks your focus on the foreign sounds. About the only reason to turn on side B audio is when you're practicing production in the “meaning → word” direction.

There's also a separate “Voice only” mode. Turn it on and the text on screen disappears, leaving only the sound. It's essentially a dictation mode. It becomes training to recall the meaning from sound alone, without leaning on the spelling, and it breaks head-on the problem of confusing “recognizing it when you see it” with “being able to recall it”. Think of it as retrieval practice with your eyes closed.

And because of Rule 1, the “apple” outside the parentheses isn't read at all. Result: no sound whatsoever.
Writing pronunciation in your native script aims at the wrong target to begin with. “アップル” is not how apple is pronounced. The moment you write sounds your language doesn't have in your own script, you end up storing a wrong pronunciation. That's exactly the loss this article has been talking about from the start.
One more trap to know about — the code looks for only the first set of parentheses. If you use parentheses twice on side A, it reads only the first and ignores the second. A card like 一日（ついたち）と一日（いちにち） reads only ついたち. One set of parentheses per card. If there are two readings, split it into two cards (exactly the same conclusion as the “one concept per card” principle).

Shadowing — how many seconds should the playback gap be?

Shadowing is the practice of immediately repeating aloud what you hear. It's where TTS delivers the most value, and also where most people fail. The reason is simple — there's no time to repeat. Run auto play at the default 3-second interval and, as soon as the audio ends, it jumps to the next card.

So you need to calculate the time. The time needed for side A of one card breaks down like this.

Time needed for side A
  = audio length × number of repeats
  + your reaction time            (about 0.3–0.5 s)
  + time to repeat it aloud       (≈ audio length)
  + margin                        (about 0.5 s)

To be upfront — of these, the only values set by the code are “playback interval, speed and repeat count.” Figures such as “0.3–0.5 s reaction time” and “0.25–0.35 s per syllable” are rough estimates from real use, not measurements. Don't take them as exact numbers — use them as a starting point. Adjusting them to your own pace is the right approach.

The audio for a single English word (2 syllables) runs roughly 0.6–0.8 s at 1.0× speed. A short example sentence (6–8 words) runs roughly 2.5–3.5 s. Let's do the math.

SituationCalculationTime neededDoes 3 s work?
Word played once + repeat aloud 0.8 + 0.4 + 0.8 + 0.5 ≈ 2.5 s Yes (the default happens to fit)
Word played twice + repeat aloud 0.8×2 + 0.4 + 0.8 + 0.5 ≈ 3.3 s No — you get cut off
Sentence once + repeat aloud 3.0 + 0.4 + 3.0 + 0.5 ≈ 7 s Nowhere near enough

That's why the app has separate delay settings for side A and side B. You leave the control panel's base interval alone and give extra time to side A only (set it to 0 and it's the same as the base interval). Since you shadow only on side A, there's no reason to lengthen side B.

Here are recommended combinations by purpose. Don't use them as-is — treat them as a starting point.

PurposeSpeedRepeatsTotal time on side AWhy
First exposure (imprinting the sound)1.0×25 s Room to hear it twice and repeat once
Shadowing (words)0.9×14 s Hear once, repeat immediately

Tone drills (Chinese)0.7×36 s Slower speech spreads the tone contours apart so you can hear them.
Don't go below 0.7

The core of shadowing is “speaking out loud.” Repeating in your head isn't shadowing. Only by actually moving the muscles of your mouth do you build articulatory motor memory — and that's what eventually becomes speaking. If you're not going to open your mouth, there's no reason to lengthen the interval. Just leave it at 3 seconds and do lots of rounds.

The limits of browser TTS — honestly

This is the most important part of the article. Browser speech synthesis has the overwhelming advantage of being free and requiring no installation, but it comes at a price. Using it with that knowledge is different from using it without.

① Voices differ by device — this is the biggest limit

The app doesn't create voices. It only borrows the voices already installed on your device. So the exact same CSV and the exact same settings can sound natural on your phone and robotic on your PC.

EnvironmentSituation
iPhone / MacHigh-quality voices are built in. Depending on the language, though, you may need to download them separately in Settings.
Android / ChromeGenerally good quality. But without the language pack, a voice for that language doesn't exist at all.
Windows + EdgeUses high-quality online voices. Often the best combination.
Windows + ChromeOften exposes only old local voices, so it sounds noticeably robotic.

That's why the app has a feature for choosing the voice yourself for each locale. The default is “Auto” (the browser picks). If you don't like the sound, drop Auto and pick one from the list yourself. A much better voice is usually hiding there.

Chinese and Thai learners: don't skip this item. In other languages, poor voice quality ends at “a bit awkward,” but in a tonal language, poor voice quality means “the information you need to learn is lost.” You can't learn tones from a voice that smears the tone contours. Choosing the voice isn't optional — it's essential.

② TTS is always off after a reload (this isn't a bug)

The app deliberately doesn't save the TTS state. It saves every other setting, but not this one. That's because browsers block sound from playing without user interaction. If it were saved as on, the app would try to make sound the moment the page opened, get blocked by the browser, and you'd hit the worst possible state: “it's on, but there's no sound.”

So it always starts off. The click you make on the TTS checkbox is itself the key that unlocks the browser's sound lock. Having to turn it on yourself every time is inconvenient, but the alternative is worse.

③ The voice list arrives late

The browser loads the list of available voices asynchronously. Right after the page opens, the voice selection list may look empty. The app refreshes it automatically once the list arrives, but if the settings window is empty, wait a moment and open it again.

④ Offline, voices change or disappear

Local voices work offline, but many of the high-quality voices are cloud-based and need a network. That's why, on a plane, the voice suddenly turns robotic or stops entirely. The app and your decks themselves work normally offline (they're stored only in the browser). Only TTS depends on the OS and the network.

⑤ A single word has no context

This problem has come up several times already, and it can't be solved structurally. English read, Chinese 行 (xíng to go / háng row, bank), Russian замок (castle / lock) — TTS has no choice but to guess, and it's often wrong.

There's only one fix: put context on the card. Either force the pronunciation with parentheses, or put an example sentence on side A. This is a limit of TTS, not something you can fix with settings.

⑥ A word on its own is read in “dictionary form” — not real-world pronunciation

This is the most underrated limit. When TTS reads a single word, what comes out is a careful, clearly enunciated citation form. But in real conversation, words aren't pronounced that way.

  • Weak forms — to isn't “too” but /tə/ “tuh,” and can isn't “kan” but gets reduced to /kən/ “kun.”
  • Linking and contraction — want to → “wanna,” going to → “gonna.”
  • Flapping — in American English, the t in water shifts to something close to /d/.

That's why it's common for someone who learned words perfectly with TTS to understand not a single word of a conversation between native speakers. The sounds you learned from word cards and the sounds flowing by in real life are different. Word-level TTS only gives you “a starting point for pronunciation” — it doesn't complete your listening. What completes it is real content (dramas, podcasts, lectures). This app can't replace that.

⑦ TTS gives input only — nobody tells you whether your pronunciation is right

This is the fundamental limit of shadowing. Playing you the correct sound and checking whether the sound you make is correct are completely different things. The app can't do the latter. Even if you repeat it wrong, no one points it out. And people can't hear their own pronunciation accurately.

The remedy lies outside the app — record your own voice on your phone and listen to it alternately with the TTS. Doing just this one thing lets you hear differences you'd never notice on your own. If possible, having a person check you is the surest way.

Summary

TTS isn't “a nice add-on to switch on” — depending on the language, it's a feature where “if you don't switch it on, you only learn half.”

  • Chinese and Thai — learn without TTS and half of each word (the tone) never gets into memory. Take care of voice quality too.
  • English, French, Japanese and Russian — the spelling betrays the pronunciation. TTS keeps wrong pronunciations from being stored.
  • Spanish and Italian — the spelling alone gives you the pronunciation. Use TTS for adapting to speed, not for pronunciation.
  • Notation in the CSV — Japanese: yomigana in parentheses; English: respell heteronyms only; Chinese: no parentheses on side A, pinyin on side B. Never put IPA or native-script pronunciation in parentheses.
  • Shadowing — if you'll open your mouth, give side A 4–5 s (7–8 s for sentences). If you won't, leave it at 3 s and do lots of rounds.
And the most important thing — TTS only “plays” the pronunciation for you; it doesn't memorize it for you. Exposure and retrieval are different. Hearing a sound doesn't mean you can produce it. You have to open your mouth. No tool can do that part for you.
Scope and limits of this article
The app behavior, setting ranges and calculation rules in this article (speed 0.5–2.0×, repeats 1–20, volume multiplication, parenthesis handling, 13 locales) are values taken from VOCA DECK's actual code.
On the other hand, “0.25–0.35 s per syllable,” “0.3–0.5 s reaction time” and the recommended combinations by purpose are rough estimates from real use, not measured research results. Individual differences are large, so use them only as a starting point.
The pronunciation explanations for each language are based on the standard variety (Putonghua, Tokyo Japanese, standard French, etc.), and regional variants and dialects may differ. And browser TTS is not a native speaker. For pronunciation in exams or anything important, always cross-check with dictionary audio and real native-speaker material.
