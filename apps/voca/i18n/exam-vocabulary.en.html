Deck Design Strategy by Exam

Last updated: 2026-07-14 · By Y-Systems

Someone who has memorized 3,000 words gets the top grade (Grade 1) in CSAT English, yet keeps missing TOEIC Part 5 questions. Someone with a TOEIC score of 900 can’t produce a single word in the TOEFL speaking section. It isn’t because they memorized too few words. It’s because each exam demands a fundamentally different kind of vocabulary skill. This article breaks down exactly what that “different kind” is, and then designs the front and back of your CSV cards differently for each exam.

The bottom line first — card format per exam on one page

This is a long article, so the conclusion comes first. Each row of the table below links straight to the matching section. You only need to read the row for the exam you’re preparing for.

ExamCore demandCard frontCard back
CSAT English Inference from context (recognition) A single English word One main meaning. Don’t list several meanings
TOEIC Collocations (chunks) The whole phrase Meaning of the phrase + the distractor words
TOEFL · IELTS Production (speaking · writing) Meaning in your native language (reversed direction) English word + its word forms
HSK Tones · character forms Characters only (no parentheses) (pinyin) meaning — the parentheses are the key
Civil service · transfer exams Past-paper frequency (my own mistakes) A single English word Meaning + why I got it wrong

If this table looks odd — especially if “no parentheses on the HSK front” seems to come out of nowhere — the reason is in the HSK section. VOCA DECK’s read-aloud treats parentheses specially, and if you don’t know that, your Chinese cards quietly break.

Vocabulary skill isn’t one thing — four axes

“Knowing an English word” actually lumps together four different abilities. Exams pick only some of them to measure. Which axis an exam measures is your card blueprint.

Axis 1 — recognition ↔ production

Seeing mitigate and thinking “to ease” is a completely different ability from having mitigate pop out the moment you want to say “to ease”. The first is recognition, the second is production. And no amount of recognition turns into production on its own. Exams with only reading and listening require recognition; exams with speaking and writing require production.

On a card, this axis shows up as direction: word,meaning or meaning,word. That one swap multiplies the difficulty several times over.

Axis 2 — word ↔ chunk

You can know what reservation means and still not know whether it’s make a reservation or do a reservation. Which verbs go with which nouns — this is called collocation. It’s knowledge separate from a word’s meaning, and knowing the meaning never leads you to it.

On a card, this axis shows up as the unit on the front: do you put a single word there, or the whole phrase?

Axis 3 — form ↔ sound

In English you can more or less get by knowing only the spelling. But in Chinese, drop the tone and you have a different word. mā(妈, mom) and mà(骂, to scold) aren’t two words with the same spelling and different sounds — they are different words to begin with. A card without sound is a card with half of it missing.

On a card, this axis shows up as TTS settings. For some exams audio is optional; for others, without audio the card doesn’t work at all.

Axis 4 — general frequency ↔ exam frequency

Words that are common across English as a whole and words that come up often on this exam are different sets. invoice, reimbursement and itinerary are rare in everyday conversation, yet they come up again and again on TOEIC. Conversely, civil-service English exams feature advanced words you almost never use in daily life.

On a card, this axis shows up as “what goes into the deck” — your selection criterion. This is what explains why dumping an entire off-the-shelf 5,000-word list into a deck usually fails.

The four axes are the four parts of a card.
Axis 1 (recognition/production) → direction · Axis 2 (word/chunk) → front unit · Axis 3 (form/sound) → TTS · Axis 4 (frequency) → selection criterion.
Once you know which axes an exam weighs, the card designs itself.

CSAT English — why the simplest card is enough

The English section of Korea’s College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT, “Suneung”) is almost entirely reading. And when a test-taker meets an unknown word while reading, they don’t need to reconstruct its dictionary definition. They only need to know which way the sentence is going.

Suppose you don’t know curtail in the following sentence.

Faced with a shrinking budget, the city had to curtail
several public programs it had launched the year before.

Grab the earlier information shrinking budget and the compulsion in had to, and the sentence itself tells you that curtail belongs to the “reduce · scale back” family. You don’t need to pin down exactly whether it means “cut”, “stop” or “scale down”, because the answer choices don’t demand that level of resolution.

So the rule for CSAT cards is “don’t raise the resolution — raise the count”. Instead of spending time perfecting a word by attaching five meanings to it, making five times as many cards with one main meaning each translates directly into points.

Handling words with several meanings — don’t touch the front

The problem is polysemous words. In CSAT passages, address appears far more often as “to deal with (a problem)” than as “street address”. subject is used as a trap meaning “likely to undergo” rather than “topic”, and sound as “valid” rather than “noise”.

At this point many people add a marker on the front — something like address (issue). Don’t do this in VOCA DECK. If the front contains parentheses, read-aloud speaks only what’s inside them. The screen shows address (issue), but the speaker says only the gloss “issue”. (The rule was originally made for reading Japanese furigana, and used unknowingly it backfires like this. The right way to use this rule is covered in the HSK section.)

The fix is to leave the front alone and turn any word that needs its secondary meaning into a phrase. That removes the parenthesis problem, and as a bonus you memorize the context along with it. 

curtail,to reduce · to cut back
address the issue,to deal with the problem
be subject to,to be liable to · to be subordinate to
a sound argument,a valid argument
compromise,to make concessions · to impair (quality)
prone,likely to · inclined to
lucrative,profitable

Front: English, no parentheses. Back: one main meaning, two at most. TTS can be on or off — the CSAT does have a listening section, but that listening is at the sentence level, not the word level, so it isn’t something word cards prepare you for. The CSAT card is the simplest card in this article. That’s correct.

TOEIC — why you miss questions when you know every word

TOEIC Part 5 has one especially frustrating type of wrong answer: you know the meaning of all four choices and still get it wrong. That isn’t a lack of vocabulary; it’s because TOEIC isn’t measuring meaning. What TOEIC measures is “which words this word goes with”.

Anatomy of a wrong answer ① — right meaning, wrong partner

The manager will _______ a meeting with the new client.
(A) arrange   (B) organize   (C) prepare   (D) settle

Anyone who memorized all four words as roughly “prepare · organize” wastes 20 seconds here and ends up guessing. Because they only memorized meanings. arrange a meeting is a fixed pairing, while prepare a meeting sounds off (it should be prepare for a meeting). This difference isn’t written anywhere in the meaning. Unless you memorize the pairing, there’s no way to know it.

Anatomy of a wrong answer ② — when the preposition decides the answer

All prices are subject _______ change without prior notice.
(A) for   (B) to   (C) of   (D) with

Even people who memorized subject as “likely to undergo” get stuck here. The answer depends on whether you know the chunk be subject to as a whole. A word card will never catch this; a phrase card catches it in one go.

Anatomy of a wrong answer ③ — when one noun decides the document type

In a Part 7 passage, if reimbursement appears, it’s an expense-claim document. If itinerary appears, it’s a business-trip schedule. If tenant appears, it’s about a lease. Knowing the meaning of these words isn’t enough; you need to carry the domain knowledge “when this word appears, this is the situation” to read passages quickly.

That’s why the front of a TOEIC card should be a “phrase”, not a word.
reservation,booking ← this card is nearly useless on the TOEIC.
make a reservation,to book ← this card solves the question.

Example TOEIC cards

The key is to put the whole phrase on the front and also write the “trap words” on the back. If you note the wrong choices that confused you, you’ll filter them out instantly the next time you meet them. 

arrange a meeting,to set up a meeting (not prepare — prepare FOR a meeting)
place an order,to order (not do an order)
meet the deadline,to make the deadline (not keep)
be subject to change,may change (preposition to is fixed)
in accordance with,according to · in line with (rules/procedures context)
take effect,to come into force (of rules)
on a regular basis,regularly
reimbursement,expense refund (if it appears: expense-claim document)
itinerary,trip schedule (if it appears: travel/business-trip context)
complimentary,free of charge (don’t confuse with complementary)

Here, parentheses on the back are safe. Back-side read-aloud deletes what’s inside parentheses before speaking (the exact opposite of the front-side rule). So a card like to set up a meeting (not prepare — ...) shows the whole trap note on screen, while the audio cleanly says only “to set up a meeting”. Make active use of back-side parentheses on your TOEIC cards.

The examples above are not quotes of real exam questions but original examples that reproduce question types. Copyright in official questions belongs to the testing organizations.

TOEFL · IELTS — what changes when you reverse the direction

TOEFL and IELTS include speaking and writing. That single fact rewrites the whole design of your deck. On a reading test, the word appears on the screen first, and you only have to recognize it. On a speaking test, nobody shows you the word. You have to pull it out of your own head.

So you have to reverse the card direction: not word,meaning but meaning,word. This is much harder. To be honest, even with the same 100 words, the meaning→word direction takes much longer and fails much more often than word→meaning.

But that difficulty is exactly what the exam measures.
Cards that go by easily don’t build production ability. The cognitive effort in the moment you try to recall and fail is what builds memory, and the word→meaning direction hardly has that moment. The meaning→word direction has it on every card.

Production cards don’t have just one thing on the back

To produce a word in speaking or writing, its word forms have to come out with it. You recall “to ease” and pull out mitigate, but what your essay needs might be the noun form in mitigation measures. Someone who memorized only the verb can’t finish the sentence here.

So the back of a TOEFL/IELTS card holds the word + its word forms + usage together. Don’t be afraid of a long back. On this exam, the back is your production material. 

to ease · to lessen,mitigate — mitigation (n.) / mitigating factors
to make worse,exacerbate — exacerbate the problem
to prove · to back up,substantiate — substantiate a claim with evidence
widespread,prevalent — prevalence (n.) / be prevalent among
to be due to,be attributed to — attribute A to B
decisive · crucial,pivotal — play a pivotal role in
on the other hand (contrast linker),whereas / conversely — opens an essay’s contrast paragraph
to conclude,in conclusion / to sum up — signal word for wrapping up in speaking

Look at the last two cards. They aren’t words but “linking words”. What earns points in TOEFL/IELTS writing isn’t difficult vocabulary but signal words that give paragraphs structure. These belong only in an exam-prep deck, and never in a CSAT deck. That’s what “designing per exam” means.

Reverse the direction, and you must reverse the audio settings too

Here’s a practical detail people miss. When you reverse the direction, the front becomes your native language. VOCA DECK sets the voice language separately for the front and the back. Leave the defaults as they are, and it will mangle the front (your native language) trying to read it with a foreign-language voice.

In Advanced settings, change the front language to your native language and the back language to English. Speed (0.5–2.0×) and volume are also adjustable separately for front and back, so slowing the back (English) down a little helps with production practice. Reverse only the direction without changing the audio settings, and the card works only halfway.

HSK — without sound, the card doesn’t work

For the exams so far, TTS was a nice-to-have. HSK is different. In Chinese, the tone is part of the word. If you memorize mai without its tone, you can’t tell whether it’s 买(to buy, mǎi) or 卖(to sell, mài). They mean the opposite. A Chinese card without sound is a wrong card.

So for HSK cards, turning on TTS isn’t an option but a prerequisite. And this is where you need to understand VOCA DECK’s parenthesis rule exactly. If you don’t, your cards quietly break.

The parenthesis rule — front and back are opposites

PositionWhen it meets parenthesesResult
Front Reads only what’s inside 美しい（うつくしい） → reads only “うつくしい”
Back Deletes what’s inside, then reads (dǎ zhé) to give a discount → reads only “to give a discount”

This rule was originally created for Japanese kanji readings (furigana). Write 美しい（うつくしい） on the front, and the screen shows the kanji while the audio gives the correct reading. For JLPT prep, you can use this rule exactly as it is.

But apply it as-is to Chinese and it breaks

It worked for Japanese, so it should work the same way for Chinese — many people think so and build cards like this. 

打折(dǎ zhé),to give a discount        ← ✗ don’t do this

Here’s what happens. Following the front-side rule, the audio reads only the dǎ zhé inside the parentheses. But dǎ zhé is romanization, not Chinese characters. Throw romanization at a Chinese speech engine and the tones come out wrong, or it simply spells the letters out one by one. The TTS you turned on to learn Chinese ends up not reading Chinese. And most people spend weeks like this without noticing.

The optimal HSK card — send pinyin to the back, in parentheses

The answer is to take pinyin off the front and move it inside parentheses on the back. Then both rules work for you at the same time. 

打折,(dǎ zhé) to give a discount — 打折商品 discounted goods
环境,(huán jìng) environment — 保护环境 to protect the environment
提供,(tí gōng) to provide — 提供服务 to provide a service
差不多,(chà bu duō) almost the same
接受,(jiē shòu) to accept (distinguish from 接收 to receive)
买,(mǎi) to buy — 3rd tone
卖,(mài) to sell — 4th tone · the opposite of 买

What actually happens when this card plays:

  • Front — the screen shows 打折, and the audio reads the characters with their tones in Chinese. (There are no parentheses on the front, so it reads everything.)
  • Back — the screen shows all of (dǎ zhé) to give a discount — 打折商品 discounted goods. Your eyes check the pinyin, while the audio removes the parentheses and reads only “to give a discount”, cleanly.

Your eyes see the pinyin, your ears hear the characters pronounced, and you confirm the meaning in your own language. Whether you know the parenthesis rule backwards or correctly is what makes this difference. Naturally, you also need to set the front voice language to Chinese in Advanced settings.

To be honest — this tool can’t teach you to write characters

At higher HSK levels, a writing (composition) section is included, and characters are presented without pinyin. The required skills then add “the ability to write the characters yourself”.
The flashing method of looking at a screen can’t build that. You get neither stroke order nor muscle memory. If your level requires writing, you must practice writing by hand separately from card memorization. What this tool covers is recognition and pronunciation. We won’t promise more than that.

One more thing. VOCA DECK’s audio uses the speech engine built into your browser as is. So depending on your device and browser, a Chinese voice may not be installed at all. Before building an HSK deck, check with one or two cards that sound actually plays. If it doesn’t, an HSK deck won’t do its job on that device. You’ll need another device or browser.

Civil service · transfer exams — you don’t “build” the deck, you “extract” it

For the previous four exams, the card format was the issue. Korea’s civil-service and university-transfer exams are different. Here the issue is what goes into the deck. The card format is almost the same word,meaning as the CSAT, but the way you choose words is completely different.

What sets this group of exams apart is that there are overwhelmingly many past papers, and the vocabulary repeats. And most test-takers make the same mistake here — memorizing an off-the-shelf book like “5,000 Civil-Service English Words” in order, starting from number 1.

This fails because, of those 5,000, half are words you already know. The time spent skipping past known words eats up half of your study. Seeing an unknown word 10 times beats seeing a known word 100 times.

The extraction procedure — your wrong-answer log is your deck

  1. Do the past papers first. You don’t memorize a deck and then do past papers; you do past papers and then build the deck. The order is reversed. That’s the key point.
  2. Write down only the words that stopped you. The spot where your hand stopped while reading a passage, the spot where you guessed on the choices. Only those words go on the list. 30–50 per pass is plenty.
  3. Write down why you got stuck. This is the card design unique to this group of exams. Did you not know the meaning, did you know it as a different meaning, or did you know the meaning but miss the preposition? Different kinds of failure call for different remedies.
  4. Mark the words you got wrong two or more times. As you go through more passes, the 20–30 words you keep getting wrong reveal themselves. That’s where the real points are.

Example civil-service · transfer exam cards

ubiquitous,found everywhere (wrong on all 3 passes)
redundant,unnecessary · laid off (missed it — didn’t know the second meaning)
abstain,to refrain · to abstain from voting (abstain FROM — missed the preposition)
tenacious,persistent · relentless
exacerbate,to worsen (synonym of aggravate — they appear together)
scrupulous,meticulous · conscientious (also unscrupulous = unprincipled)
alleviate,to relieve (synonym group with relieve · mitigate)
inadvertently,unintentionally · by accident (adverb-form trap)

See how “why I got it wrong” goes in the parentheses on the back? These parentheses aren’t read aloud; they only appear on screen. So you hear the meaning cleanly while retracing the memory of the failure with your eyes.

The real value of this deck is that it’s small.
A 150–200-card deck extracted from 3 passes through past papers pays off in your score more directly than an off-the-shelf 5,000-word list. The former consists of words from questions you actually got wrong; the latter consists of words someone gathered because they “might come up”.

What you lose when you reuse one deck for a different exam

“I have a deck I made for the CSAT. Can’t I just use it for the TOEIC?” You can. Just know what survives and what disappears.

TransferWhat survivesWhat dies
CSAT deck → TOEIC Recognition of core vocabulary. Passage-reading speed All collocations. Word cards can’t handle Part 5 vocabulary questions. Business vocabulary (invoice · itinerary) is missing too
TOEIC deck → TOEFL A feel for phrase-level units. Formal vocabulary All production ability. The direction is word→meaning, so nothing comes out in speaking or writing. Academic vocabulary is also lacking
CSAT deck → civil service About half the vocabulary (an estimate). Same card format The advanced vocabulary layer. And without “my own mistakes” as the selection criterion, it’s full of words you already know
English deck → HSK Nothing Everything. When the language is different, the very idea of reuse doesn’t apply

In the table above, “about half” is a rough figure that varies widely from person to person. Don’t trust the number. What you should trust is the direction — what dies in a transfer is always “the axis unique to that exam”. A CSAT deck lacking collocations isn’t an accident; they were never put in because the CSAT doesn’t measure collocations.

A realistic recommendation: don’t reuse a deck wholesale — split it into two layers. Keep the core vocabulary layer (word cards that stay valid when the exam changes) as is, and build the exam-specific layer (collocations, production direction, past-paper mistakes) anew for each exam. If you split your decks into small pieces, this separation becomes much easier.

So what should my cards look like — four questions

You don’t even need the exam’s name. Answer the four questions below and the card format follows. Exams not on this list (JLPT, TEPS, G-TELP, terminology for professional certifications and so on) can be designed with this procedure too.

Question 1 — do I have to “produce” this word, or just “recognize” it?

If there’s speaking or writing (including written short answers), you have to produce it → meaning,word direction.
If it’s only reading, listening and multiple choice, recognizing is enough → word,meaning direction.
If you need both, make two decks with the same words. One card can’t hold both directions.

Question 2 — do wrong answers split on “meaning” or on “partners”?

Pick apart just 5 past-paper questions and you’ll know. If the choices have different meanings → word cards are enough.
If the choices all mean roughly the same and only one sounds natural → make the front a phrase. TOEIC is this case; the CSAT isn’t.

Question 3 — is it a language where sound changes meaning?

Chinese (tones), Japanese (multiple readings of the same kanji) → TTS required + design around the parenthesis rule.
English → TTS is a supplement. Nice to have, and the card works without it.
If you answered “yes” here, check that your device actually plays sound before building the deck.

Question 4 — where will the words come from?

Exams with many, repetitive past papers (civil service, transfer exams, various certification exams) → extract from your past-paper mistakes. No off-the-shelf lists.
Exams with a broad scope and fresh passages every time (CSAT, TOEFL) → lay a foundation with a high-frequency word list, then keep adding the words that actually stopped you — that’s the realistic approach.

String the four answers together, and that’s your card.
e.g. “must produce + splits on partners + sound irrelevant + extracted from past papers” → a situation in your native language on the front, an English phrase + word forms on the back, collected only from past-paper mistakes. No off-the-shelf vocabulary book has this combination. Only you can make it.

What this article can’t do — honest limits

We believe an article that states its limits is better than one that sells a metric. So here they are.

① The five-exam classification itself is rough

Even within one exam, different parts demand different things. TOEIC LC demands sound; RC demands partners. IELTS Academic and General have different vocabulary layers. The five boxes in this article are a starting point, not an answer key. In the end, you have to apply the four questions to your own past papers yourself.

② You can memorize all the vocabulary and still not get the score

Words are a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. Time management, grammar, logical structure, question-type drills, listening-comprehension speed — a sizable part of the score lies in areas a deck can’t touch. Don’t bet 100% on your deck. A deck is a tool for clearing a bottleneck, not a tool for solving the exam.

③ There are things this tool clearly can’t do

  • Writing Chinese characters — stroke order and muscle memory don’t come from a screen. Write by hand.
  • Personalized spaced repetition (SRS) — VOCA DECK cycles through cards evenly. It doesn’t automatically show difficult cards more often. Instead, you filter them yourself with “memorized marks”.
  • Question practice — cards leave you with words, but the feel for solving questions only comes from questions.
  • Voice quality — it uses the browser’s built-in voices as is. Quality varies widely by device, and voices for some languages may be missing.

④ Exam formats change

The exam characteristics described here are our judgment of general question trends at the time of writing. Testing organizations change formats, scoring and section structure. Always check the official guidelines of the exam you’re applying for.

One last line

A deck should be a miniature of the exam. If the exam asks about partners, your cards should be partners; if the exam makes you produce, your cards should make you produce; and if the exam asks about sound, your cards should make sound.

Even with the same word list, the front and back of your cards should differ depending on the exam. Making that difference takes 30 minutes. Skip those 30 minutes, and you’ll spend months diligently training the wrong skill.
