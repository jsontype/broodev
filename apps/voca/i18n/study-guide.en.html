Vocabulary study strategy

Last updated: 2026-07-14 · Written by Y-Systems

Even with a good tool, using it the wrong way just burns time. This article gives concrete answers to “How many words a day?”, “When should I review?” and “Why do I keep forgetting no matter how often I look?”, along with a sample 4-week plan.

The biggest time-waster: “rereading”

Most people memorize words by reading them over and over. They highlight, read aloud, and read again. It feels productive, but it is the least effective method there is.

The reason is the familiarity trap. After seeing a word many times, its letters start to look familiar, and your brain mistakes that familiarity for “I know this.” But in the exam room you have to recall it from a blank page. Recognizing something and recalling it are completely different skills.

What to do instead: retrieval practice
Look at the word and try hard to recall its meaning before you see it. It is fine if you fail — the very effort of trying strengthens the memory. Checking the answer comes after that.

The same goes for the Flashing Memorizer. In the brief moment before the meaning appears, consciously try to guess the answer. This one habit turns passive exposure into active learning. For the underlying principles, see How it works.

How many words a day is right?

There is no single right answer, but most people set the number too high. Aim for “100 words a day” and you quit after 3 days. Sustainability is what determines how much you learn in total.

GoalNew words/dayRealistic?
Light maintenance10 – 15Very sustainable. 4,000 words in a year
Serious study20 – 30Good for exam prep. Review load needs managing
Short-term sprint40 – 50Doable for a few weeks, but risks burnout
“100 a day”100+Almost always fails. Reviews explode and everything collapses

The key point is that reviews quickly far outnumber new words. Add 20 new words a day, and within a few days more than 100 words are waiting for review. When deciding how many new words to add, base it on whether you can also handle the reviews.

The most common collapse pattern — In week one you enthusiastically add 100 words a day. By week 2, 500 reviews have piled up. You can’t keep up. You give up.
The person who starts slow and keeps going wins.

When should you review?

According to the forgetting curve, forgetting is fastest at the beginning. So reviewing just before you would forget is the most efficient. And each time you review, you can lengthen the gap until the next review.

A rhythm like this is widely used.

RoundWhenPurpose
1stSame day as learning (a few hours later)Block the steep early forgetting
2ndThe next dayCheck what is left after a day
3rd3 days laterWiden the interval
4th1 week laterMove into medium-term memory
5th2–4 weeks laterLock into long-term memory

These numbers are an example of a rhythm, not strict rules. Being off by a day or two is fine. What matters is the principle of “spaced out, several times”, not the exact dates.

How to do it with the Flashing Memorizer — one pass on the day you create a new deck, another pass the next day, another 3 days later… Simply repeat the same deck over several days. If you remove the cards you definitely know with the “Memorized” mark, the deck gets lighter with each round and your remaining time goes to the hard words.

Why you should mix things up (interleaving)

If you always repeat in the same order, the order itself becomes a cue. You have memorized a chain — “after apple comes banana” — not the words themselves. When a word pops up on its own in real life, you can’t recall it.

Shuffling the order removes these false cues, so every time you do genuine retrieval. It feels harder at first — but that difficulty is the learning. Learning that feels easy is usually not very effective.

A sample 4-week plan

A realistic plan based on 15–20 minutes a day and 20 new words.

WeekWhat to do
Week 1 Create deck A (100 words). Each day, 20 new words + review that day’s words again the same day. At the weekend, one full pass through deck A.
Week 2 Create deck B (100 words). Each day, 20 new words + review deck A (skipping the ones marked as memorized). Deck A gets steadily lighter.
Week 3 Create deck C. Review deck B + deck A only 1–2 times a week. Shuffle is a must.
Week 4 Create deck D. Review deck C + a weekly check of decks A and B. Collect only the words that still haven’t stuck into a separate “hard deck”.
The “hard deck” is the key. After about 4 weeks, 20–30 words you keep getting wrong will reveal themselves. Gather just those into a separate CSV and you have your most efficient word list. Seeing an unknown word 10 times beats seeing a known word 100 times.

Mistakes people keep making

  1. Setting the goal too high. 100 words a day → giving up after 3 days. 20 a day for 1 year adds up to far more.
  2. Adding only new words and never reviewing. It’s like filling a bucket with a hole in it. A new-to-review ratio of about 1:3 is natural.
  3. Just looking passively. If you don’t try to recall before seeing the meaning, exposure is not learning.
  4. Seeing words you already know again and again. Filter them out with the Memorized mark. If you don’t, half your time is wasted.
  5. Spending all your time building the word list. Starting today with 30 words beats building the perfect 5,000-word deck.
  6. Cramming it all into one day. 15 minutes a day for 12 days sticks far longer than a 3-hour cram session (the spacing effect).
In short — a little at a time, often, recalling, and mixed up.
These four points are the conclusion vocabulary research has confirmed again and again over the past 100 years. A tool only removes friction; the “effort to recall” is up to you.
