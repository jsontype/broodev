Leitner · SM-2 · FSRS, and why we chose not to use an algorithm · Last updated: 2026-07-14 · Written by Y-Systems

The heart of spaced repetition is “who calculates the review date, and how” — and we chose not to do that calculation at all. 1972 — Leitner: “moving the box is the algorithm”

The problem of that era

There were no computers. What the German journalist Sebastian Leitner proposed in his 1972 book was spaced repetition that needs no calculation. No pencil, no date arithmetic. The physical act of moving a card is itself the saved state.

How it worked

  • Divide a box with partitions into 5 compartments.
  • Every new card starts in compartment 1.
  • Answer correctly and the card moves forward one compartment.
  • Get it wrong and, no matter which compartment it was in, it drops straight back to compartment 1.
  • Each compartment has its own review cycle. A commonly used arrangement looks like this.
CompartmentReview cycleWhat being here meansWhen you miss
No. 1Every dayNot known yet / just missedStays in No. 1
No. 2Every 2 daysAnswered correctly once→ drops to No. 1
No. 3Every 4 daysTwo correct in a row→ drops to No. 1
No. 4Every 8 daysThree in a row→ drops to No. 1
No. 5Every 16 daysAlmost memorized→ drops to No. 1

Strictly speaking, Leitner’s original scheme was capacity-based — “empty a compartment when it is full” — not date-based. The 1·2·4·8·16-day schedule above is a calendar version that settled in later. Many articles blur this distinction, so we note it here.

What broke

Leitner’s fatal weakness is that “the fall is too brutal.” A card in compartment 5 that slips just once is treated exactly the same as “a new card never answered correctly” and drops to compartment 1. In reality that card is almost memorized, but the box has no way to express that. With only one integer of state, there is nowhere to store “almost memorized, but confused today.”

And every card climbs the same ladder. An easy word must step through all 5 compartments, and a word that simply won’t stick also gets exactly 5. What was needed was a ladder each card climbs at its own speed. That arrived 15 years later.

1987 — SM-2: 15 years to turn an integer into a real number

The problem of that era

SM-2, published by Poland’s Piotr Woźniak while building SuperMemo, replaces Leitner’s “compartment number (integer)” with an “easiness factor (real number)”. The key invention is a single value: the EF (Easiness Factor).

How it worked — the actual formula

At every review, SM-2 has you grade yourself on six levels, q = 0~5 (5 = perfect, 0 = total blank). It then updates EF like this.

EF' = EF + ( 0.1 − (5−q) × (0.08 + (5−q) × 0.02) )
initial EF = 2.5   ·   EF floor = 1.3 (never goes below this)

Here are the values this formula actually produces. We filled in this table by hand calculation.

q (self-grade)MeaningEF changeStarting from EF 2.5
5Recalled perfectly+0.102.60
4Correct, after a brief hesitation±0.002.50 (unchanged)
3Correct, barely−0.142.36
2Wrong, but recognized it on seeing the answer−0.322.18
1Wrong, answer still unfamiliar−0.541.96
0Complete blank−0.801.70

That q=4 comes out at exactly 0 is the design intent of this formula. “Correct without trouble” is a neutral point that leaves the difficulty estimate unchanged; do better and EF goes up, do worse and it goes down. The constants 0.1 · 0.08 · 0.02 were not derived from theory — Woźniak tuned them by hand. This becomes important later.

The next interval is then set like this.

I(1) = 1 day
I(2) = 6 days
I(n) = I(n−1) × EF        (n ≥ 3)

if q < 3 → reset repetition count n to 1 (interval returns to 1 day)

EF is a multiplier. So an easy card at EF 2.5 and a hellish card at EF 1.3 diverge exponentially. With the same number of reviews, they reach completely different intervals.

Review no.EF 2.5 (easy card)EF 1.9 (average)EF 1.3 (hellish card)
1st1 day1 day1 day
2nd6 days6 days6 days
3rd15 days11 days8 days
4th38 days22 days10 days
5th94 days41 days13 days
6th235 days78 days17 days
7th587 days (1.6 years)148 days22 days

At the 7th review it is 587 days versus 22 days — a 27-fold gap. This is where SM-2 beats Leitner. It sweeps easy cards out of sight for a year and a half at a time and spends all that time on the hard cards instead.

What broke

① Six-level self-grading is too hard for humans. For every card you have to decide the difference between q=3 and q=4. In practice most users keep pressing the same two or three grades. The six-level design effectively collapses into three.

② The failure handling is ambiguous. The original text says “if q<3, start repetitions from the beginning without changing EF,” yet the pseudocode in the same document updates EF for every q. Implementations interpret it differently. That a key branch point of an algorithm used for over 30 years is ambiguous on paper is something it is more honest to state plainly. (The simulation in this article takes the “EF is updated too” reading.)

③ The constants are fixed without justification. Why is I(2) 6 days? Why 0.08 of all numbers? Numbers hand-tuned by one person in 1987 on his own data are applied unchanged to every learner in the world. That is why FSRS came along.

The Anki variant — and “ease hell”

Anki does not use SM-2 as-is. It cuts the grades down to four (Again / Hard / Good / Easy), calls EF ease, and re-tuned the size of the adjustments. Here is how it actually behaves with the default preset.

ButtonEase changeNext intervalNotes
Again (fail)−0.20Previous interval × 0% → minimum 1 day
(+ 10-minute relearning)		Interval reset to the floor
Hard−0.15Previous interval × 1.2Fixed multiplier independent of ease
Good±0.00Previous interval × easeNeutral point (SM-2’s q=4)

Other defaults: learning steps 1m 10m · graduating interval 1 day · easy interval 4 days · starting ease 2.50 · ease floor 1.30 · leech threshold 8 lapses. Anki’s defaults can vary by version and preset, so check them yourself in the deck options.

What “ease hell” is — a card on which you keep pressing Again (−0.20) and Hard (−0.15) gets stuck at the ease floor of 1.30. From then on, the next interval grows only × 1.3 at a time, so it crawls 1 day → 2 days → 3 days → 4 days… On top of that, Again knocks the interval back to 1 day. The result is that a handful of cards occupy your daily review queue forever.
The only path that raises ease is Easy (+0.15), and you never press Easy on a hellish card. Four doors lead down and only one leads back up — this is the structural flaw of the SM-2 family, and the reason Anki users say “8 leech cards are ruining my life.”

The remedies mostly lie outside the algorithm. Delete the card, split it, add a sentence to it (“the card is bad, not me”), or reset its ease by hand. It means people are fixing by hand what the algorithm cannot fix, and that realization gave birth to the next generation.

FSRS — the only algorithm that can answer “what exactly is S?”

FSRS (Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler) throws away hand-tuned constants and fits its parameters to your actual review log. It is based on the DSR model.

  • D (Difficulty) — how inherently hard this card is
  • S (Stability) — how long the memory holds up
  • R (Retrievability) — the probability of recalling it right now

It defines the forgetting curve like this (as of FSRS-4.5 / 5, DECAY = −0.5, FACTOR = 19/81).

R(t, S) = ( 1 + (19/81) × t/S ) ^ (−0.5)

One fact almost nobody points out

Let’s substitute t = S. In other words, “what happens if you see a card with stability S exactly S days later?”

R(S, S) = ( 1 + 19/81 ) ^ (−0.5)
        = ( 100/81 ) ^ (−0.5)
        = ( 81/100 ) ^ ( 0.5)
        = 0.9   ← exactly 90%

This is no coincidence. FACTOR = 19/81 and DECAY = −0.5 are constants worked backward to produce exactly this value. So what S really is can be defined in one sentence.

S (stability) = “the number of days it takes for the probability of recall to fall to 90%”
S = 30 means “if you meet this card again in 30 days, you will get it right nine times out of ten.” It is not an abstract internal variable but a physical quantity you can read in days. SM-2’s EF allows no such interpretation. Ask what “EF 2.5” means and the only answer is “a number you multiply by.”

How review volume changes with desired retention — a conversion table

FSRS lets you set the desired retention directly (default 0.90). Solving the formula above for t gives the interval. 

interval t = S × (81/19) × ( R^(−2) − 1 )

Here are the results of plugging numbers into that formula. This table may be the most practical part of this article.

Desired retention RInterval (multiple of S)For a card with S=30 daysReview load
0.95× 0.4614 daysSurges about 2.2×

How to read it: raise retention from 0.90 to 0.95 — “a mere 5 points” — and the interval shrinks to less than half, doubling your reviews. Conversely, lower it to 0.80 and your reviews fall below half, at a cost of only 10 points of accuracy. Because the curve is this steep, the intuition that “higher retention is always better” collapses here. Unless an exam is right around the corner, the 0.85~0.90 range is generally sensible.

Accuracy note: the derivation above is based on FSRS-4.5 / 5, where DECAY is fixed at −0.5. From FSRS-6 onward, DECAY itself is a learned parameter, so the identity “S = the 90% interval” may not hold exactly. The number of parameters has also grown by version, 17 → 19 → 21. Don’t quote these numbers as-is without checking the version.

What FSRS can’t do

FSRS only becomes yours once it has your log. Until a few hundred to roughly 1,000 reviews pile up, it runs on default parameters, which are ultimately “someone else’s average.” In other words, FSRS in your first month is not personalized. And keeping a log also means that every review record is stored somewhere.

VOCA DECK dissected — what our code actually does

Now it’s our turn. We write it exactly as it is in the code, with no packaging.

What a card stores: this is all of it

{ a: "ubiquitous", b: "present everywhere", mem: false }

That is the whole of one card. Front, back, and a single boolean. We store neither when you reviewed it, nor how many times you missed it, nor how much it confused you.

So even if we wanted to bolt on SM-2, we couldn’t. SM-2 needs at minimum EF · repetition count · last review date, and FSRS needs the full review log to fit its parameters. We have no accounts, no server and no review log. Your deck and memorized marks live only in the browser’s localStorage and are never sent to a server. This isn’t something we failed to build — it’s how we built it. The price is giving up personalization.

What the scheduler really is: one line of modulo arithmetic

The way we decide the “next card” is a cyclic index. It splits three ways depending on the order setting.

SettingBehaviorRe-encounter intervalCoverage
fwd Forward (default)Index cycles by +1Exactly every N cards (even)100% guaranteed
rev BackwardIndex cycles by −1Exactly every N cards (even)100% guaranteed
rnd RandomIndependent uniform draw at every step
(excluding only the current card)		Expected N−1 cards,
high variance		No guarantee ✗
Random mode is not a shuffle — this is something we need to state clearly.
Rather than shuffling the cards and going around once (sampling without replacement), it draws anew each time from the remaining cards. So the same card can come up three times in a row, or not appear even once during a whole lap.

We calculated the price in numbers

Suppose you run a 100-card deck (N=100) in random mode for 99 steps (= one forward lap’s worth). The probability that a particular card is drawn on a given step is 1/(N−1) = 1/99. So the probability that the card never appears during those 99 steps is: 

P(0 appearances) = (1 − 1/99)^99 ≈ e^(−1) ≈ 0.37

The distribution of appearance counts closely follows a Poisson distribution (λ=1).

Appearances during one lap’s worth (99 steps)ProbabilityMeaning
0About 37%A third of the deck never shows up
1About 37%As intended
2About 18%Wasted
3 or moreAbout 8%The same card over and over

In other words, run one lap’s worth in random mode and about 37% of the deck never even shows its face that day. Even after two laps’ worth (198 steps), about 13% still hasn’t appeared, and only after three laps’ worth does the unseen share drop below 5%.

So use it like this (a prescription that comes straight from the code)
  • First pass · full check the day before the exam → Forward. These are the moments you need 100% coverage. Run it in random and you’ll skip a third.
  • From the third pass on, when you need to break order cues → use Backward first. It breaks order cues while keeping coverage at 100%. Most people walk right past this option.
  • Genuine random retrieval training → Random. But use it prepared to run three laps’ worth of the deck.

Time unit: our scheduler’s tick is “seconds”

The default delay is 3 seconds (1~60 seconds in the control panel). Each card goes through two phases, front and back, so it’s 6 seconds per card by default. The timer also has a floor of 0.5 seconds (however short you set it, it never goes below that).

This immediately yields a physical upper limit on deck size.

Flashing Memorizer’s honest limit: split decks into 300 cards or fewer. Build a single 2,000-card deck and one lap takes 3 hours 20 minutes. That is no longer “rapid repeated exposure.” It’s just slow list reading. If you need to manage 2,000 cards, that’s Anki’s job, not this app’s.

The delay is the length of your “retrieval window” — set it asymmetrically

If you think of the delay as just “speed control,” you’re using only half of it. The few seconds the front is on screen are the only time you can struggle to recall the answer. That’s why we made it possible to set the front and back delays separately (advanced settings, 0~60 seconds).

The “front 5 s / back 1.5 s” of passes 2~3 is the setting in this app closest to retrieval practice. As your passes progress, lengthen the front and shorten the back. Most people do the opposite, and end up just passively watching all the way through.

What the “Memorized” mark really is — a Leitner box with only 2 compartments

Our only personalization mechanism is a single thing: the Memorized mark. Translated into the language of algorithms, it is exactly this.

Memorized mark = a Leitner box with 2 compartments. But it is a door, not a ladder.
Box 1 = mem: false (keeps cycling) · Box 2 = mem: true (removed from the cycle)
And the Range setting chooses the box — Skip memorized (default) / Memorized only / All.

What you get from this alone (more than you’d think)

The biggest share of what a personalization algorithm does is actually “not showing you cards you know.” When SM-2 pushes an EF 2.5 card 587 days out, it too is ultimately trying not to show it now. The Memorized mark captures the crudest but biggest chunk of that effect.

Let’s look at the numbers. In a 100-card deck, mark 40 cards as memorized and 60 cards remain in the cycle. One lap shrinks from 10 min → 6 min, and the re-encounter interval for the cards you don’t know shrinks from 10 min → 6 min. In the same 20 minutes, you meet the hard cards 1.67 times as often. A free 1.67×. From a single checkbox.

Where it falls short of real SM-2 (this is the important part)

① There are no levels — it’s 0 or 1. SM-2 distinguishes “EF 2.5 (very easy),” “EF 1.6 (somewhat hard)” and “EF 1.3 (hell)” and shows them at different frequencies. We only have “show / don’t show.” Within the 60 unmemorized cards, everything is perfectly equal. A word you saw for the first time yesterday and a word that won’t stick after 40 viewings come up at exactly the same frequency. This is the clearest point where we lose to SM-2.

② There is no time — so “when to see it again” can’t be calculated. mem is a boolean. We don’t store when you marked it. So a judgment like “it’s been 40 days since this card was marked, time to check it again” is something the app fundamentally cannot make. There’s no timestamp.

③ The resulting blind spot — marked cards quietly rot.

This is the most important warning in this article.
The Memorized mark is a one-way door. Once a card goes out to box 2, there is no way for it to come back on its own. SM-2 will always call even a 587-day card back eventually — to catch forgetting. We never call it back.
So a state of “a card you marked as memorized has actually been forgotten, and you don’t know it” keeps piling up. And you find out in the exam room.

The procedure to fill that blind spot by hand

Since the algorithm won’t do it, a person has to. Fortunately it isn’t hard, and the app already has the feature. Switching the Range to “Memorized only” exists for exactly this purpose.

  1. Pick one day a week. (e.g. Sunday)
  2. Switch the Range to “Memorized only.” → Only the cards you marked as memorized cycle.
  3. Set the order to Random and the front delay long, 4~5 seconds. — You’re testing genuine retrieval.
  4. When a card comes up that you can’t recall, unmark it on the spot. → Demoted to box 1. You are opening the door back by hand.
  5. Switch back to “Skip memorized” and run as usual.

These 5 steps are the whole of manually imitating SM-2’s “mature card reviews.” Not being automatic makes it inferior, but it is overwhelmingly better than doing nothing. Skip this, and the Memorized mark becomes “a feature for hiding cards you only pretend to know.”

The most accurate answer: the two work on different layers

If you’ve read this far, the following sentence should now make sense.

Anki decides “which cards to look at today.” (in days)
Flashing Memorizer decides “how to expose those cards right now.” (in seconds)
The two don’t fight over the same problem — they stack on top of each other.

Here is the combination people who use both actually rely on.

  1. Leave the long-term schedule to Anki. FSRS picks “today’s 120 cards” out of a 5,000-word deck for you.
  2. Export the cards you keep missing in Anki (leeches, ease near 1.3) to a CSV. Usually this comes to 30~80 cards.
  3. Load that CSV into Flashing Memorizer and run two or three laps a day. At 80 cards, one lap takes 8 minutes. Front 5 s / back 1.5 s for retrieval practice.
  4. Remove cards that have become solid in Flashing Memorizer with the Memorized mark, and reset their ease in Anki.

Flashing Memorizer does what Anki can’t, and Anki does what Flashing Memorizer can’t. Anki can’t create high-density exposure within a session (it won’t show you the same card 20 times a day), and Flashing Memorizer can’t calculate a review date 6 months out. A single file (CSV) links the two tools. How to make one is in Making a CSV word deck.

Short Q&A

Q. Is FSRS always better than SM-2?

Generally yes, once enough review log has built up. But not in your first month. FSRS only becomes yours after fitting its parameters to your log; until then it runs on someone else’s average. And FSRS rests on the assumption that you press the buttons honestly. Lazily press only Good, and a sophisticated algorithm will be sophisticatedly wrong. Garbage in, garbage out — and that’s something no algorithm can fix for you.

Q. What should I set desired retention to?

Look at the conversion table above again. Going from 0.90 → 0.95 doubles your review volume. You’re paying twice the time to buy 5 points of accuracy. In most cases that’s a losing trade. If the exam is far off, 0.85~0.90; if it’s right around the corner, 0.90~0.95 is a sensible starting point. But this is a “starting point,” not “the answer.”

Q. How many seconds should the Flashing Memorizer delay be?

The “right value” differs with each pass. The default of 3 seconds is merely a reasonable value for a first pass. As passes accumulate, lengthen the front (time to recall) and shorten the back (just for checking). Front 5 s / back 1.5 s is the setting closest to retrieval practice. The detailed arrangement is in the table above.

Q. Isn’t random mode always better than forward? It breaks order cues, after all.

No — that is exactly the trap this article calculated. Random doesn’t guarantee coverage (about 37% unseen per lap’s worth). If you want to break order cues while keeping coverage, use Backward. Most users don’t know why this option exists.

Q. I’ve marked everything as memorized and my deck is empty. Am I done?

No. That’s where it begins. Switch the Range to “Memorized only” and check once a week. Unmark the cards you couldn’t recall to send them back. Skip this procedure and the Memorized mark becomes a feature that hides forgetting. The procedure is written above.

Q. So will you add SM-2 to VOCA DECK someday?

To add it, we’d have to store a review log; then storage grows, you’d want cross-device sync, and then accounts become necessary. At that moment this app’s one strength, “zero friction,” disappears. That would be rebuilding Anki as a worse version of Anki. We are not trying to become Anki. For now we have no plans to add it, and we have laid out the reasons for not adding it throughout this entire article.

Finally — an algorithm can’t make you open the app

FSRS is the best scheduler in the world. And if you don’t open the app for 3 days, it is nothing. It just sits there, having perfectly spaced “340 cards to review today,” waiting, and the person who sees that number closes the app.

The 50-year history of spaced repetition algorithms has been “a race to minimize reviews.” Leitner with 5 compartments, SM-2 with EF multiplication, FSRS with your own log. All aimed at the same goal — erasing unnecessary reviews.

But that is not where people actually fail. People don’t quit because “there are too many reviews”; they quit because “they didn’t open it today.” That is why we gave up personalization entirely and bet everything on bringing friction down to zero. We are not sure it was the right choice. But it is the choice we made, and in this article we have hidden none of its price.
