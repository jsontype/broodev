The Math of Drawdowns and Staged Buying

Last updated: 2026-10-10 · By Y-Systems · This article consists of calculations only

Model version note — The indicator count (6), weights and worked examples in this article come from the v1 model, before the two on-chain indicators (MVRV Z and Thermocap Z) were added. The dashboard score now uses v2, with 8 indicators and different weights; see the scoring methodology for the current figures.

There is no advice in this article. Only calculations. Using nothing but numbers, it shows why “it has fallen a lot, so it's cheap” is dangerous, and what staged buying actually does for you — and what it does not. Let me state the conclusion up front — staged buying is not a technique for raising returns. It is a technique for narrowing the range of outcomes. The nine-cell table below proves it.

Before we get into the calculations, let's look at what the protagonist of this article — “drawdown from the all-time high” — has actually looked like over BTC's history. The 0% line means new highs are being set, and every valley is a stretch where the “required recovery” from the tables below had to be paid.

Calculation 1 — The asymmetry of breaking even

Let's start with the simplest and cruelest formula. For an asset hit by a drawdown d, the gain needed to get back to the original amount is not d. 

Required gain = d ÷ (1 − d)

Hit by −50%      0.50 ÷ 0.50 = +100%
Hit by −80%      0.80 ÷ 0.20 = +400%

On the way down, losses come off the amount you still have; on the way up, gains build on the amount that has shrunk. Because of this single difference in the denominator, losses and recoveries are never symmetric. Here is the full table.

−10%+11.1%₩9MAlmost symmetric. Up to here, intuition holds
−20%+25.0%₩8MThe gap starts to open
−30%+42.9%₩7MWhere our model switches on “watch”
−40%+66.7%₩6MDown 40, but recovery takes +67. Already 1.7×
−50%+100.0%₩5MWhere our model sees “strongly buy-favorable”
−60%+150.0%₩4MNeeds 2.5×
−70%+233.3%₩3M3.3×
−80%+400.0%₩2M5×. You have to wait out an entire cycle
−90%+900.0%₩1M10×
−95%+1,900.0%₩0.5M20×. Practically a different asset at this point

What to notice in this table is not the values but the gaps. As the drawdown deepens by 40pp from −10% to −50%, the required recovery grows from 11% to 100% — 89pp. But from −50% to −90%, deepening by the same 40pp, the required recovery grows from 100% to 900% — 800pp. The same 40pp, nine times the damage. Drawdowns grow linearly, but their cost grows explosively.

Here is the first practical implication. “It has already fallen a lot, so falling further won't make much difference” is a complete misconception. Going from −50% to −70% is not “20pp more down” — it is an event where the recovery burden goes from +100% to +233%, 2.3 times as much. Each further decline is always more expensive than the one before.

Calculation 2 — Declines don't add up, they multiply

The second trap is that people calculate drawdowns by addition. “Hit by −30%, then another −30%, so −60%.” No. Drawdowns multiply. 

Cumulative drawdown = (1 − d₁) × (1 − d₂) × … − 1

−30% twice  →  0.7 × 0.7 = 0.49  →  −51%
−50% twice  →  0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25  →  −75%  (needs +300% to recover)
Consecutive declinesIf you add themActual cumulative drawdownTo break even
−20% × 5−100% (total loss?)−67.2%+205%
−30% × 2−60%−51.0%+104%
−30% × 3−90%−65.7%+191%
−50% × 2−100%−75.0%+300%

Being multiplicative is both good news and bad news. The good part: even five hits of −20% don't take you to zero — multiplication only approaches zero, never reaches it. The bad part: the bounces along the way give back far less than you'd think. After a −50% hit, even a +30% bounce gives 0.5 × 1.3 = 0.65 — still −35%. Why the relief of “it's finally bouncing” is betrayed so often in a bear market — the answer lies in this multiplication.

Calculation 3 — Why the 365-day high, of all things

To measure a drawdown you have to decide where it fell from. Change the reference point and the same price becomes a different drawdown. Our model uses the 365-day high, not the all-time high (ATH). That choice comes at a price, and this section spells it out.

Suppose a coin hit ₩2 million three years ago, its highest price within the last year was ₩1 million, and it now trades at ₩600,000.

Reference pointDrawdown calculationResultWhat this number says
All-time high ₩2M60 ÷ 200 − 1−70%“Historic crash zone”
365-day high ₩1M60 ÷ 100 − 1−40%“Deep correction relative to the past year”

Same coin, same price, numbers that differ by almost a factor of two. We chose 365 days for one reason: a drawdown measured from the ATH stops being an indicator and becomes a constant as time goes on. If you anchor to the 2021 peak forever, some coins will show “−70%, undervalued” for five years straight. An indicator that gives a buy signal every day is an indicator that carries no information.

The price — a 365-day window resets itself in a long bear market. If a decline lasts more than a year, the 365-day high itself moves down into the declining stretch. Say the price fell from 100 → 30 over 14 months. Now the high of “the last year” is no longer 100 but, say, 55. The drawdown is calculated as 30 ÷ 55 − 1 = −45%. Against the ATH it is −70%, yet our indicator says −45%. There are stretches where the drawdown score looks milder precisely when the price is at its deepest. This is not a bug but a structural limitation of the window approach. You need to know it when you use it.

The −30% and −50% thresholds follow the same logic. Crypto routinely sees −30% corrections even in the middle of a bull market. So −30% does not mean “buy”, it means “start watching” (recovery burden +43%, still manageable). −50%, on the other hand, is where recovery needs +100%, and historically it has shown up more often at cycle turns than in bull-market corrections. Both thresholds are set not by price but by “the point where the recovery burden doubles.”

Calculation 4 — The same ₩12 million, three ways

Now to the main event. You have a budget of ₩12 million. One coin initially costs ₩1 million. There are 9 observation points, t0 through t8 (think of them as months). We compare three ways of deploying the money.

  • Strategy A — Lump sum. Buy with the full ₩12 million at t0.
  • Strategy B — Four equal tranches. ₩3 million each at t0, t2, t4 and t6. Mechanically, regardless of price.
  • Strategy C — Drawdown-weighted tranches. Split the ₩12 million into 12 units of ₩1 million, and deploy only when the drawdown from the 365-day high first enters each band:
Drawdown bandUnits deployedAmountCumulative spent
0 to −20%0 units₩00%
−20 to −35%1 unit₩1M8.3%
−35 to −50%2 units₩2M25.0%
−50 to −65%4 units₩4M58.3%
Below −65%5 units₩5M100%

These bands and unit allocations are an example to illustrate the calculation, not a recommendation of any particular allocation. What matters is not the numbers themselves but the shape: “allocate more the further down you go.”

Judged by average cost alone, the ranking is clear: C (51.21) < B (58.81) < A (100.00). The drawdown-weighted approach cut the average cost to almost half of the lump sum's. This is where most articles end with “See? Staged buying is the answer.” We don't stop here.

First, something has to be pointed out: Strategy C did not manage to spend the ₩12 million. It put in only ₩7 million. The very premise of “investing the same total” does not hold for the drawdown-weighted approach. If the market doesn't fall deep enough, budget is left over. That is not a side effect; it is the definition of the strategy. So a fair comparison has to look at total assets including the leftover cash.

Please read these nine cells slowly. No strategy comes first in all three scenarios.

  • Strategy C wins decisively in ① and ②. And comes last in ③. A 0% return. It sat on cash and watched while the market rose 68%.
  • Strategy A wins only in ③. And overwhelmingly so (+68%). But in ② it takes −71%. According to the table in Calculation 1, that is a hole that needs +245% just to break even.
  • Strategy B is first nowhere, and last nowhere.

The rightmost column is the conclusion of this entire article. Range of outcomes: A 139pp, B 83pp, C 41.5pp. The finer you split and the more you weight by drawdown, the more the best case gets shaved and the worst case gets lifted. What staged buying does is not raise the average but narrow the distribution. This isn't a matter of taste; it's a result of arithmetic. It is a trade in which you buy the downside by giving up the upside.

“Staged buying makes you more money” is wrong. “Staged buying makes you die less” is right. C beat the others in ① and ② partly because I drew two falling paths and only one rising path. Add a second rising path and the average ranking flips. Change the paths and the ranking changes. The only thing that doesn't change is that the “range” is ordered A > B > C, and that is the only result of this simulation you can trust.

Calculation 6 — The arithmetic of running out of ammo

Where most people who say they buy in stages actually fail is not the allocation table. It is spending everything too high up. Let's nail this down with numbers.

We add Strategy D. It captures the common impulse of being scared and “wanting to get in fast.” As soon as the decline starts, it puts in ₩4 million each at t1 (−12%), t2 (−26%) and t3 (−38%) and exhausts the budget at −38%. We calculate it on Scenario ① (which then falls further to −62% before recovering to 61).

Same decline, same budget, same “staged buying.” Yet the average cost splits into ₩731.6k and ₩512.1k. The difference is ₩219.5k — a full 30%. The P&L is −16.6% versus +11.1%, 27.7pp apart.

What Strategy D did wrong was not failing to split. D also split its buying into three. What D did wrong was running out of money before the cheapest stretch arrived. In the simulation, D didn't buy a single coin at the ₩380k bottom. The price of spending the whole budget at −38% is watching −62% from the sidelines.

The one rule of ammo allocation — the further down you go, the more you need to have left. This is why Strategy C's allocation (0 · 1 · 2 · 4 · 5 units) is not an arithmetic progression but grows toward the end. As the drawdown deepens, the quantity the same money buys grows explosively. ₩1 million buys 1.35 coins at a price of 74 and 2.63 coins at a price of 38 — almost twice as many. The most powerful buying always happens in the scariest stretch, and if your balance is zero by then, that stretch might as well never have existed.

Calculation 7 — What “no floor” means

Every calculation so far has rested on one unspoken assumption: that the asset survives. Break that assumption and every table above flips.

Let's plug the typical path of a coin headed for delisting straight into the calculation. 8 time points, starting at a price of ₩1 million.

Price (₩10k)1007045251251.50.3

Look at the bottom row. From t2 to t7, the whole time the asset was dying, the drawdown indicator gave a perfect score. “Strong buy” at −55%, “strong buy” at −95%, “strong buy” at −99%. The drawdown indicator was not right even once here. It was 100% consistently wrong. And because its structure “tells you to buy harder the further it falls,” the more wrong it was, the bigger the mistake.

Let's run the three strategies along this path. The final price is ₩3,000.

A Lump sum12.00 coins₩1.00M₩36k−99.7%+33,200%
B Four equal tranches234.67 coins₩51.1k₩704k−94.1%+1,595%

Two things emerge here. First, all three strategies died. Second, the “best-performing” strategy scored −94.1%. Bring back the formula from Calculation 1, and even this best result needs +1,595% to break even. There is no winner in this table. Only losers who fared less miserably.

Look at Strategy C. The drawdown-weighted approach was so superior in Scenarios ① and ②, yet here it followed the “it's cheap” signal, poured 91.7% of the budget into the −55% and −75% zones, and ended at −99.1%. Weighting by drawdown means “buy more the further it falls,” and on an asset heading to zero, that is an algorithm for maximizing losses. The very property that made C the winner in Scenario ① is what sentences C to death here. It is the same property.

The precondition of a drawdown indicator — “this asset will still exist five years from now.” Drawdown assumes that price will revert. For an asset that will revert, it is a good indicator; for an asset that will disappear, it is the most dangerous indicator. And the chart can't tell the two apart. A −80% Bitcoin chart and a −80% chart of an altcoin on the verge of delisting look exactly the same. The difference lies not in the indicator but in fundamentals, liquidity, the team and token unlocks — and our model looks at none of these. The thousands of altcoins that have since vanished all, without exception, passed through the −90% zone, and in every one of those stretches the drawdown indicator gave a perfect score.

Calculation 8 — That's why we give drawdown only 18% and 14%

Calculation 7 above is why, in our model, drawdown is not the top-weighted of the 6 indicators in either mode.

ModeDrawdown weightRank among 6Top indicator
Short-term (momentum)18%3rdRSI 28% · F&G 28%
Long-term (contrarian)14%Tied 5thRSI · MACD · F&G · Mayer 18% each

The surprising part is that it is actually lower in long-term mode (18% → 14%). “Isn't long-term investing exactly where you should be watching drawdown?” you might think — but the reason is double counting.

Drawdown and the Mayer Multiple essentially ask the same question — “Is the price now below some past baseline?” Drawdown simply uses the 365-day high as its baseline, and Mayer uses the 200-day moving average. In long-term mode the Mayer Multiple sits in the top group at 18%. Giving drawdown 18% as well would pile 36% onto the same perspective (valuation). The reason for using 6 indicators is to blend different perspectives, and that would break the design. So in long-term mode we give weight to Mayer, which uses a more stable baseline (the 200-day average), and lower drawdown, which depends on a single high. The 365-day high is a value from a single day; if that day was an abnormal spike, the drawdown for the whole following year is distorted. A 200-day average has no such weakness.

Drawdown alone cannot produce a STRONG BUY

Let's confirm with a calculation what these weights mean in practice. With drawdown at a perfect score (100), its contribution to the total is:

Short-term: 100 pts × 0.18 = 18 pts contributed (the other 82% comes from the other 5 indicators)
Long-term:  100 pts × 0.14 = 14 pts contributed (the other 86% comes from the other 5 indicators)

To clear the STRONG BUY (80 pts) threshold, the weighted average of the other 5 indicators must be

  Short-term:  (80 − 18) ÷ 0.82 = 75.6 pts or more
  Long-term:   (80 − 14) ÷ 0.86 = 76.7 pts or more

In other words, even if a −70% crash gives drawdown a perfect score, unless RSI, MACD, the Fear & Greed Index, Mayer and the cross average above 75, the score will never reach 80 (STRONG BUY).

To take it to the extreme — if drawdown alone scores 100 and the other five indicators all score 0, the total is 18 in short-term and 14 in long-term. Both fall in the 0–25 band, so the long-term tab shows OVERHEATED and the short-term tab shows AVOID (strong downtrend). “It's down 70% and the long-term tab says overheated?” It looks odd, but it is exactly the intended behavior. Only the price has fallen; the trend is dead, sentiment hasn't improved, and it's still expensive relative to the 200-day line — that is exactly what the delisting path in Calculation 7 looks like. Keeping drawdown alone from overpowering the other five — that is why the 18%/14% weights exist.

Note: for coins listed less than a year, there is no 365-day high, so drawdown is missing. Where these calculations lie

So far I've shown you eight tables. Now here are the reasons you shouldn't trust them.

  • I made up the three scenarios. They are hypothetical paths, not a backtest. Change the numbers even slightly and the strategy ranking can flip any way you like. The only thing this simulation proves is not “C is good” but “the more you split, the narrower the range of outcomes.”
  • Average cost is not a performance metric. In Calculation 7, Strategy B's average cost was ₩51.1k, the lowest of the three. And it ended at −94.1%. A low average cost doesn't mean you bought cheap; it only means you kept buying while it fell. If the asset dies, the result is zero no matter what your average cost is.
  • Fees, slippage and taxes were all set to zero. In reality, staged buying involves more trades and so pays more in fees. Part of the differences in the tables above gets shaved off in real life.
  • Opportunity cost was set to zero. Strategy C's “0.0%” in Scenario ③ assumes the leftover cash earns nothing. Add deposit interest and it looks a little better; conversely, if other assets rose during that year, it is a real loss.
  • A 365-day window makes drawdowns look shallower in a long bear market (the warning in Calculation 3). The indicator can become least sensitive in the deepest stretch.
  • Strategy C's bands and unit allocation (0·1·2·4·5) are an example for the calculation. We do not recommend this allocation, nor do we claim it is optimal. Change the bands to −25/−40/−55 and the results change too. There is no right answer.
  • The most important variable isn't in the calculation. Will this asset exist five years from now? That answer lies not in a table but in the substance of the project, and our model doesn't look at it.

To sum up: drawdown does not measure “how cheap.” It only measures “how far it has fallen.” The two mean the same thing only when the asset survives; when it doesn't, they mean the exact opposite. The moment this indicator shouts “buy” the loudest cannot be told apart from the moment the asset is dying — that is everything you need to know about drawdown.
