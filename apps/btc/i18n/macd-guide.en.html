Calculating MACD by Hand
— A Walkthrough That Follows 42 Closing Prices to the End

Last updated: 2026-10-10 · By Y-Systems · This article is not an explanation but a hands-on lab record

Model version note — The indicator count (6), weights and worked examples in this article come from the v1 model, before the two on-chain indicators (MVRV Z and Thermocap Z) were added. The dashboard score now uses v2, with 8 indicators and different weights; see the scoring methodology for the current figures.

Let me start with a failure. Take 30 closing prices and run the standard MACD (12·26·9) on them: you get 5 MACD line values, 0 signal line values and 0 histogram values. With a month’s worth of data you cannot produce even one MACD histogram bar. Surprisingly few people know this, because most learned MACD only as “the difference between the 12-day and 26-day lines.”

So this article does not start with a definition. We plug in 42 actual closing prices and follow EMA12 → EMA26 → MACD line → signal line → histogram → broodev sub-score one bar at a time, calculator in hand. With nothing more than a calculator you can reproduce every number here, and if you can’t, we are the ones who got it wrong.

Before the walkthrough, here is the calculation this article covers applied to real BTC data. It is exactly the same formula as the 42-price exercise below (EMA12−EMA26, signal EMA9).

STEP 1

Why EMA instead of SMA — “a bar from 12 days ago creates today’s signal”

Textbooks say “EMA is more sensitive to recent prices, so it’s faster” and stop there. That sentence is only half right. Using the same 42 closes, we ran an SMA-based MACD (SMA12−SMA26) and an EMA-based MACD (EMA12−EMA26) side by side.

Result 1 — the EMA bottomed one bar earlier

The EMA version hit its low (−7.008) on day 28 and turned; the SMA version did so on day 29 (−7.201). A one-bar difference. On top of that, the EMA low is shallower (−7.008 vs −7.201). So far, exactly as the textbook says.

Result 2 — but the SMA got back to the zero line faster

Look at day 42. The SMA version is at −0.006, effectively through the zero line, while the EMA version is still at −1.389. This is where “EMA is always faster” breaks down. So why did the SMA suddenly jump?

From day 41 to day 42 the SMA-based MACD line moved from −1.036 to −0.006, +1.030 in a single day. On the same day the EMA version moved only +0.565 — nearly a twofold difference. Do the arithmetic and the cause appears. 

SMA12(day 42) − SMA12(day 41)
  = (new day-42 close 86.2 − day-30 close 74.3 that left the window) ÷ 12
  = 11.9 ÷ 12
  = +0.9917          ← the effect of "the day-30 bar disappearing," not of today's price

Change on the SMA26 side = (86.2 − day-16 close 87.2) ÷ 26 = −0.0385

Change in SMA-based MACD = +0.9917 − (−0.0385) = +1.030   ✅ matches the table

This is the real reason not to use SMA. Most of the SMA signal on day 42 did not come from today’s price rising; it came from the low of 74.3 from 12 days earlier (day 30) suddenly dropping out of the moving-average window. Nothing happened in the market today, yet something that happened 12 days ago creates today’s buy signal. The industry calls this the “drop-off effect.”

An EMA has no window. The weight of old prices just fades gradually, exponentially, as (1−α)ⁿ; prices are never suddenly evicted from the calculation one day. That is why the EMA version moved smoothly, +0.565, on the same day.

In short — the reason to use EMA is not that it is “faster” but that “it doesn’t create ghost signals.” The SMA is one bar slower at turning points, and in exchange it scatters fake jumps created by a bar from 12 days ago at random moments. What matters is not a signal’s speed but its cause.

STEP 2

Punching EMA12 into a calculator

The EMA broodev uses takes the simple average of the first period values as its seed and updates exponentially from there. α = 2 ÷ (period + 1). For EMA12, α = 2/13 = 0.153846. 

// broodev's actual code (apps/btc/app.jsx)
function ema(values, period) {
  const alpha = 2 / (period + 1)
  let seed = 0
  for (let i = 0; i < period; i++) seed += values[i]
  let prev = seed / period          // ← seed = simple average of the first period values
  out[period - 1] = prev
  for (let i = period; i < values.length; i++) {
    prev = values[i] * alpha + prev * (1 - alpha)
    out[i] = prev
  }
}

Now we feed in the 42 closes above. A seed appears on day 12, and updating begins on day 13.

Day 12 seed = (100.0 + 99.1 + 100.4 + ... + 91.7) ÷ 12
            = 1162.7 ÷ 12 = 96.8917

Day 13 = 90.3 × 0.153846 + 96.8917 × 0.846154 = 95.8776
Day 14 = 91.1 × 0.153846 + 95.8776 × 0.846154 = 95.1426
Day 15 = 88.6 × 0.153846 + 95.1426 × 0.846154 = 94.1360
Day 16 = 87.2 × 0.153846 + 94.1360 × 0.846154 = 93.0689
Day 17 = 88.0 × 0.153846 + 93.0689 × 0.846154 = 92.2891

The whole idea is simply “take 84.6% of yesterday’s EMA and add 15.4% of today’s close.” The weights are the same every day, and the past is diluted by 84.6% every day. The weight of a close from 30 days ago is 0.153846 × 0.846154³⁰ ≈ 0.0009 — less than 0.1%. But it is not zero. That difference was the conclusion of STEP 1.

STEP 3

EMA26 and the MACD line — the first value only appears on day 26

For EMA26, α = 2/27 = 0.074074. The seed is the simple average of days 1–26.

Day 26 seed = 2341.4 ÷ 26 = 90.0538
Day 27 = 76.4 × 0.074074 + 90.0538 × 0.925926 = 89.0425
Day 28 = 75.1 × 0.074074 + 89.0425 × 0.925926 = 88.0097
Day 29 = 76.0 × 0.074074 + 88.0097 × 0.925926 = 87.1201
Day 30 = 74.3 × 0.074074 + 87.1201 × 0.925926 = 86.1704

Because the EMA26 seed first appears on day 26, the first value of the MACD line (EMA12 − EMA26) is also on day 26. Up to day 25, the MACD line itself does not exist. 

MACD line (day 26) = EMA12(83.106) − EMA26(90.054) = −6.948

And in this walkthrough the real price bottom was on day 30. The MACD line had already turned on day 28. The indicator changed direction two bars before price did. Keep this in mind — it becomes the basis for “why direction, not absolute value” in STEP 6.

STEP 4

The signal line — where 30 bars fall short

The signal line is the EMA9 of the MACD line — that is, an EMA of an EMA. Since the MACD line starts on day 26, the signal line’s seed (the average of 9 MACD values) can only be built once days 26 through 34 are all in. 

Signal seed (day 34)
 = average of the 9 MACD line values, days 26–34
 = (−6.9479 −6.9682 −7.0084 −6.8882 −6.8512
    −6.6404 −6.4634 −6.0835 −5.6129) ÷ 9
 = −59.4641 ÷ 9
 = −6.6071

Histogram (day 34) = MACD line (−5.613) − signal line (−6.6071) = +0.994   ← first value!

From day 35 on, we update with α = 0.2.

Day 35 signal = −5.2360 × 0.2 + (−6.6071) × 0.8 = −6.3329
              histogram = −5.2360 − (−6.3329) = +1.0969
Day 36 signal = −4.6976 × 0.2 + (−6.3329) × 0.8 = −6.0058
              histogram = −4.6976 − (−6.0058) = +1.3083
Day 37 signal = −4.0946 × 0.2 + (−6.0058) × 0.8 = −5.6236
              histogram = −4.0946 − (−5.6236) = +1.5290
Why 30 bars give zero histogram values
26 (EMA26 seed) + 9 (signal seed) − 1 = 34. The earliest possible first histogram value is on bar 34. But broodev looks at the histogram’s change versus the previous bar, so it needs 2 of them → 35 bars. That is why the code has this guard. 
if (closes.length >= 35) { ... }   // ← under 35 bars, MACD sub-score = null
When MACD is null, weight renormalization kicks in, the score is computed from the remaining 5 indicators, and it is shown as coverage 5/6. You will see this state often with newly listed coins.

One more thing: 35 bars is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. The day-34 histogram is still “half-baked,” because the signal seed is a simple average of 9 MACD values. To see a stable histogram in practice you need at least 60–80 bars. We come back to this with numbers in Appendix A at the end of this article.

STEP 6

Where broodev parts ways with the textbook — direction, not absolute value

Up to here it has been standard MACD; any charting program gives the same values. From here on is what is unique to broodev. We publish, straight from the source, the functions that turn the histogram (a real number with no fixed range) into a 0–100 score. 

// Short-term (momentum) mapping — MACD weight 14%
const momMacd = (cur, prev, px) => clamp(
    50
  + 30 * tanh( (cur / px)          * 800 )    // ① histogram level
  + 15 * tanh( ((cur - prev) / px)  * 800 )    // ② direction vs previous bar
, 0, 100)

// Long-term (contrarian) mapping — MACD weight 18%
const conMacd = (cur, prev, px) => clamp(
    50
  + 25 * tanh( ((cur - prev) / px)  * 600 )    // ② direction vs previous bar
  + 10 * tanh( (cur / px)          * 600 )    // ① histogram level
, 0, 100)

The two functions have the same structure with the coefficients flipped. That’s all there is to it.

TermMeaningShort-term (momentum) coefficientLong-term (contrarian) coefficient
① LevelHow big the histogram is right now (absolute value)3010
② DirectionWhether it got bigger or smaller than the previous bar (Δ)1525
Level : direction ratio2 : 11 : 2.5
tanh gainThe larger it is, the faster small changes saturate800600

Short-term mode weights level twice as heavily. How strong the trend is matters.
Long-term mode weights direction 2.5 times as heavily. Even if the histogram is still deeply negative, “is it better than yesterday?” dominates the score.

Same histogram, different direction — how far apart do the scores get?

With a price of 100, we fed in four situations where the histogram value is the same and only the direction versus the previous bar differs.

Compare rows 1 and 2. The histogram value is identical at −0.30. Only the direction differs. Yet the long-term sub-score spreads from 22.6 to 58.4, a gap of 35.8 points. A model that looks only at absolute value cannot tell these two situations apart. Both are “bad.”

But row 1 is exactly the day-28 spot we saw in STEP 3: the bar where, just as price was making a new low, selling pressure began to ease. Looking only at absolute value, you would have scored that bar as “the worst.” In reality it was two bars before the bottom.

Rows 3 and 4 tell the same story in the opposite direction. The histogram is positive (+0.10), so to an absolute-value model both are “good,” but one is rolling over and the other is rising. The long-term scores split into 37.5 vs 66.5.

In one sentence — the histogram’s absolute value tells you “how good things are now,” while the change versus the previous bar tells you “whether things are getting better.” From a long-term accumulation standpoint, what we want to buy is not an asset that has already gotten better but an asset that has stopped getting worse. That is why, in the long-term mapping, the direction coefficient (25) overwhelms the level coefficient (10).

The price: tanh saturates quickly (the lesson of day 41)

There is no free lunch. Weighting direction heavily means the score swings hard even on tiny noise. Day 41 in the STEP 5 table is the proof. 

Day 40: ΔHistogram = +0.155  (+0.183% of price 84.7)  → long-term score 80.0
Day 41: ΔHistogram = −0.014  (−0.017% of price 83.9)  → long-term score 57.4

Direction term:
  Day 40 → 25 × tanh(600 × 0.00183)  = 25 × tanh( 1.098) = +20.0
  Day 41 → 25 × tanh(600 × (−0.00017)) = 25 × tanh(−0.100) = −2.5
  Difference −22.5 pts   ← matches the drop in the long-term sub-score (−22.6)

A single tiny dip in the histogram, worth 0.017% of price, knocked 22.6 points off the long-term sub-score. The cause is tanh saturation. tanh(1.098) ≈ 0.80 — in other words, once Δ is just 0.18% of price, the direction term already takes 80% of its maximum (25 points). Beyond that, no matter how large it gets, it barely rises. Conversely, when the sign flips, those 20 points vanish instantly.

Seen as a table, the saturation curve is obvious.

Past 0.4%, both terms are effectively at their maximum. Anything beyond adds no information. In other words, the information that “the histogram is enormous” is barely reflected in the broodev score. Even in a melt-up, the MACD sub-score sticks near 100 and goes no higher. This is by design (so that extreme values can’t dominate the composite score), but it is also a clear loss of information. We are saying so plainly.

In 27 bars the buy/sell signal flipped 10 times. That is whipsaw. Had you followed the classic rule “buy when the histogram crosses above zero, sell when it crosses below,” you would have bought and sold 10 times in these 27 days, with the price going nowhere and only the fees paid 10 times over.

Scarier still is the amplitude of the score. The market moved only ±1.5%, yet the short-term sub-score swung from 21.1 (strong avoid) to 83.0 (strong buy). Between 21.1 on day 50 and 81.0 on day 38 there is almost no real market difference — a 2.6% difference in closing price.

So how much does the final score swing?

MACD is just one of 6 indicators. Apply the weights and compute its actual impact on the composite score (0–100).

Short-term mode: sub-score span 61.9 × weight 0.14 = 8.67 pts
Long-term mode:  sub-score span 38.4 × weight 0.18 = 6.91 pts

Here comes a counterintuitive result. Short-term has the lighter weight (14% < 18%), yet its final score swings more (8.67 > 6.91). The level coefficient of 30 and the gain of 800 make the short-term mapping far more sensitive. If you look only at the weights and conclude “short-term pays less attention to MACD,” you are wrong. You have to look at the mapping function too.

For reference, in both modes the average is near 50 (52.6 / 50.7). In a sideways market the MACD sub-score stays neutral on average. The problem is that you trade on each day’s value, not on the average.

STEP 8

Weight 14% vs 18% — answering with arithmetic

In the weights published in the methodology document, MACD is 14% short-term and 18% long-term. “Why is MACD, a momentum indicator, lighter in momentum (short-term) mode?” — a question we get often, and a fair one. The answer is redundancy.

ModeMomentum/trend characterContrarian characterMACD’s role
Short-term RSI 28% + F&G 28% + drawdown 18% + MACD 14% + cross 8% = 96%
In short-term mode RSI, F&G and drawdown are all mapped in the momentum direction		 Mayer 4% Redundant. 96% is already looking at the trend
Long-term MACD 18% + cross 14% = 32% F&G 18% + Mayer 18% + drawdown 14% = 50%
(RSI 18% uses a contrarian mapping)		 The only brake. More than half of the 32% that holds back the 68% shouting “buy”

Of the 6 indicators in short-term mode, effectively 5 are already mapped in the momentum direction. RSI is mapped as 50 + (RSI−50) × 1.6, so higher means a higher score (overbought = good); F&G also scores higher the greedier it gets, and drawdown scores higher the shallower it is. Loading MACD heavily on top of that would be counting the same information four times. On top of that, as we saw in STEP 7, MACD is the noisiest indicator in this set (10 flips in 27 bars). There is no reason to pile weight onto a noisy, redundant indicator. Hence 14%.

Long-term mode is the exact opposite. F&G (18%) · Mayer (18%) · drawdown (14%) — 50% combined is contrarian indicators, and these three are all wrong together in a bear market. All three shout “it’s cheap, buy,” while the price keeps falling. At that point the only indicators that can say “not yet” are MACD (18%) and the golden/death cross (14%). And the cross, using the 200-day moving average, is the slowest signal — near a bottom it lags by weeks.

Conclusion — MACD’s 18% in long-term mode is not “because we trust momentum more” but “because there aren’t enough brakes.” In a structure where the contrarian trio takes 50%, MACD is the only fast indicator that confirms whether the trend has actually turned. And that confirmation comes not from whether the histogram has turned positive (slow) but from whether the histogram is bigger than yesterday (fast) — which is why the direction coefficient in the long-term mapping is 25. The 18% weight and the direction coefficient of 25 are two faces of the same decision.

Appendix A

Your chart and our histogram may differ

This is something most MACD articles never tell you. MACD is not a standardized specification. “12·26·9” is fixed, but what the EMA’s first value starts from (seeding) is not.

  • The broodev approach — uses the simple average of the first period values as the seed (STEP 2·3 above)
  • Another common approach — uses the first close itself as the seed and rolls the EMA from day 1

The reason is the very tanh saturation we criticized in STEP 6 as an “information loss.” In this stretch the histogram is already large enough that the level term is pinned at its maximum, and with both saturated, the difference cannot carry over into the score.

The same property is both a weakness and a strength
tanh saturation throws away information about the size of extreme values (the downside from STEP 6). Yet thanks to that very property, the score barely moves under implementation noise such as differences in seeding method or data source (the upside from Appendix A). Gain one, lose the other. We chose robustness. Whether that was the right choice, we don’t know.

It’s too early to relax, though. The results above describe a trending stretch where the histogram is already large. If you insert the same 0.30% difference when the histogram is near 0 (= before saturation), this happens.

When the two histograms fall on opposite sides of the zero line, the short-term sub-score diverges by 50 points. And near zero is exactly where people look for signals — turning points, sideways markets, just before a cross. The stretch where seeding differences matter most happens to be the most important stretch. (Here too the long-term mapping swings far less than the short-term one: +14.3 vs +50.0, because its level coefficient is only 10.)

Three practical implications
  • Don’t compare absolute MACD values across sites. If broodev’s histogram differs from your charting program’s, neither one is wrong — they use different seeding methods. The comparison itself is meaningless.
  • Be careful the shallower the data. On newly listed coins the influence of the seed hasn’t washed out yet. On an asset like BTC with hundreds of bars accumulated, the difference is negligible.
  • Be especially suspicious of the signal “the histogram just crossed zero.” As the table above shows, near the zero line implementation differences can split the sub-score by up to 50 points. Rather than the zero-line cross itself, look for directional persistence across several bars — that is far safer.

Appendix B

What this walkthrough did not prove (and cannot)

So far we have reproduced the calculation with hypothetical data of 42 and 60 bars. Let me clearly separate what this article proved from what it did not.

What it proved

  • broodev’s MACD calculation is the standard 12·26·9, and with the numbers above anyone can reproduce it.
  • The histogram is available from bar 34, and direction from bar 35. That is why the closes.length >= 35 guard exists.
  • The long-term mapping weights direction 2.5 times as heavily, with direction coefficient 25 : level coefficient 10. That is literally what the code says.
  • The score is invariant to price scale (identical to the fourth decimal place even ×700).
  • In a sideways market this indicator flips its signal 10 times in 27 bars. That is a measured weakness of our model.
  • MACD is not a standardized specification. Merely changing the seeding method changes the signal date on the same data. Thanks to tanh saturation, however, in trending stretches that difference barely carries over into the sub-score (0.2–4.9 points). Near the zero line it can diverge by up to 50 points.

What it did not prove (important)

  • This is not proof that “watching for a change in direction makes money.” The 42 bars above are hypothetical data that I built to rebound. It would be just as easy to build data that, after a change of direction at the bottom, keeps falling another −40%. Transparency of the calculation and predictive power are entirely different problems.
  • MACD is inherently a lagging indicator. It is a moving average of the difference between moving averages, after all. By the time the histogram changes direction, part of the move is already over. The MACD line turning 2 bars before price in STEP 3 was divergence, not foresight — and divergence fails often.
  • The 14%/18% weights are the author’s judgment. The reasoning in STEP 8 (avoiding redundancy, securing a brake) is a persuasive design rationale, not proof of an optimum. 16% might be better. The data doesn’t give a clear-cut answer.
  • tanh saturation throws away extreme information. As we saw in STEP 6, once the histogram exceeds 0.4% of price, any further magnitude is not reflected in the score. It is a design that deliberately discards the intensity of historic crashes and melt-ups.
  • MACD knows nothing about news, regulation, hacks or liquidity. The histogram can turn up beautifully even while an exchange is going bankrupt.

The MACD sub-score is only 14–18% of the broodev composite score. The remaining 82–86% comes from the other five indicators, and how they are combined is fully disclosed in the methodology document. No single indicator decides anything on its own. That is the design intent.
