How to Spot a Bitcoin Bottom
— Market Cap vs Average Cost Basis

Last updated: 2026-07-22 · Author: Y-Systems · Scope: the on-chain components of the broodev bottom score (BOTTOM RADAR)

Every attempt to find Bitcoin's bottom by price has failed. The 2018 bottom was $3,150; the 2022 bottom was $15,500 — a fivefold difference. Yet the two bottoms shared one thing that had nothing to do with price. Bitcoin's market cap had fallen below the sum of what every investor actually paid (the total average cost basis). This article draws that observation directly from live data, walks chronologically through how the signal actually printed at five bottoms, and publishes, formulas and all, the weights with which broodev's bottom score (BOTTOM RADAR) blends it in.

Why the "average cost basis" tells you about bottoms — realized cap

Everyone knows market cap: current price × circulating supply. It is a number that assumes a single last traded price represents the value of every coin in existence. But on-chain data holds another number that is far more interesting than market cap: realized cap. 

Market cap    = current price × circulating supply       (every coin valued at "today's price")
Realized cap  = Σ (each coin × the price on the day that coin last moved)
              = total amount investors actually paid ≈ market-wide average cost basis × circulating supply

The blockchain records when every coin last moved between wallets. Treat the price on the day a coin moved as that coin's "acquisition cost," add them all up, and you get the total amount of money the whole market has actually put into Bitcoin. In stock terms, it is every shareholder's average purchase price multiplied by the share count. That is why this article calls realized cap the "average cost basis (total)."

The ratio of the two numbers is the famous MVRV.

MVRV = market cap ÷ realized cap

MVRV 2.0  → the average holder has doubled their money (everyone in profit)
MVRV 1.0  → the market as a whole is exactly at breakeven
MVRV 0.8  → the average holder is down −20% (everyone underwater)

This is where the logic of the bottom comes from. MVRV dropping below 1 means Bitcoin holders as a whole are, on average, underwater. Historically, two things have happened in this zone. First, capitulation by investors who can no longer stand their losses reaches its peak. Second, the buyers absorbing that supply are long-term holders who are not shaken by gains and losses. It is the moment coins pass from weak hands to strong hands, and in all four cycles that moment coincided with a major bottom. You can check it yourself in the chart below.

Notice one property of the blue line: it barely falls. Even while market cap crashes −80%, the average cost basis only drifts down gently. That is because cost is updated only when a transaction actually happens. Even in a terrified market most coins do not move, and the cost of a coin that has not moved stays the same. So the blue line behaves not like a gravitational line for price but like the market's book value. Market cap breaking below book value is rare, and because it is rare, it is a signal.

Five bottoms — the days MVRV fell below 1

In Bitcoin's history, the phases in which market cap fell below the average cost basis (MVRV < 1) can be counted on one hand. Here is the record of how they coincided with five major bottoms. All figures are approximations — they vary depending on which day you count as the low.

Each of the five bottoms had a completely different cause: the collapse of its own bubble (2011), the aftermath of Mt. Gox (2015), the unwinding of the ICO bubble (2018), the pandemic panic (2020), and the FTX bankruptcy (2022). Prices range from $2 to $15,500 — a 7,750-fold difference. And yet MVRV was below 1 all five times, mostly in the 0.7–0.9 range. Measure not by absolute price but by "how deep a discount to the whole market's cost," and five utterly different bottoms land in almost the same place.

The converse, however, does not hold. "MVRV < 1 = an immediate bottom" is not true. In 2011, in 2014–2015 and at the end of 2018, MVRV stayed below 1 for weeks to months, and prices fell further even within that window. What this indicator tells you is not "this is the lowest point" but zone information: "historically, people who accumulated in stages within this zone won big 1–2 years later." Read it as a zone tool, not a timing tool.

Live chart ② — standardizing with the MVRV Z-Score

The MVRV ratio has one weakness: the height of the peaks differs from cycle to cycle. MVRV at the 2011 peak exceeded 7, but in 2021 it fell short of 4. The amplitude compresses as the market grows. So the broodev engine uses a Z-Score instead of the ratio — a measure of how many standard deviations the difference between market cap and realized cap has opened up, relative to market cap's historical volatility. 

MVRV Z-Score = (market cap − realized cap) ÷ σ(market cap)
               σ = standard deviation of market cap over its full history

z < 0   → market cap below the average cost basis (historical bottom zone)
z ≈ 1~2 → normal range
z > 5   → historical top zone (the 2011·2013·2017 peaks)

The chart below shows the MVRV Z-Score over Bitcoin's full history. Green shading is z < 0 (bottom zone), red shading is z > 5 (overheated zone). The 2011·2015·2018·2022 bottoms all dipped below the zero line (March 2020 only grazed it), and the major tops of 2011·2013·2017 all reached the red shading at z 7–9. Yet the z at the 2021 peak stopped around 3.5 — a sign that the upside amplitude compresses as the market grows, a story we return to in the limits section.

Live chart ③ — Thermocap: the bottom according to mining cost

If realized cap is the investors' cost, Bitcoin has one more cost: the producers' cost, i.e. the cost of mining. Thermocap is the cumulative total (USD) of rewards paid to miners (block rewards + fees) since Bitcoin launched. It is the sum of all the money spent so far on securing the network — the total cost of producing this asset, so to speak. 

Thermocap    = Σ (daily miner revenue USD)  ← accumulated since 2009
Thermocap Z  = z-score of ln(market cap ÷ Thermocap)   [over full history]

Historically, the market cap / Thermocap ratio swings between several times and several tens of times.
The lower the z of the ratio's standardized logarithm, the more undervalued relative to production cost.

Market cap trading at some multiple above Thermocap is the normal state (a property of an asset in a long-term uptrend). What matters is where that multiple sits within its historical range, and standardizing the log of the multiple as a z-score makes cross-cycle comparison possible. In the chart below you can see that the stretches where z fell below 0 coincide with historical bottom zones.

Thermocap's denominator differs decisively from realized cap. It never shrinks, and it grows only a little each day regardless of the market. Investors can dump coins in a panic and the cumulative mining reward will not budge. That makes Thermocap Z a slower, more stubborn bottom baseline than indicators based on realized cap. This indicator is Bitcoin-only, since it needs mining reward data — it cannot be calculated for altcoins.

How to read the moments when the two indicators diverge

Both are "cost" indicators, so why watch both? Because their denominators move at different speeds. Realized cap (MVRV's denominator) is updated every time coins change hands — when a bull market drags on, the cost itself rises, and once capitulation ends, the cost settles lower. Thermocap has none of that; it only accumulates. This difference in speed is what creates the signal.

CombinationInterpretationHistorical examples (approx.)
MVRV Z low + Thermocap Z lowDeep bottom zone. Close to both investor cost and production cost2015 · late 2018 · late 2022
MVRV Z low + Thermocap Z normalA mid-cycle crash. Capitulation has happened, but a structural bottom is not confirmedMarch 2020 · July 2021
MVRV Z normal + Thermocap Z highLate bull market. Cost has already risen a lot, so MVRV alone gives a false pictureNovember 2021 (second peak)
Both highHistorical top zone. The worst zone for new buyinglate 2013 · late 2017

The third row matters most. When a bull market lasts more than a year, realized cap climbs along with price, and MVRV looks "normal." At the cycle high in November 2021, MVRV was far lower than at the 2011–2017 peaks. It is a silence with exactly the same structure as the Mayer Multiple calling November 2021 neutral. That is when an indicator like Thermocap, whose denominator does not climb along, serves as a cross-check.

Limits of these indicators — a 4-cycle sample, the ETF era

To be honest about it: here is how far the argument of this article holds.

  • The sample is only 4 cycles. The sample behind the observation that "MVRV < 1 was the bottom" is five phases, or four completed cycles. That is an embarrassingly small size to call statistics, and an observation is not a law. There is no guarantee the next cycle will stop in the same place.
  • Possible distortion in the ETF era. Since 2024, large amounts of coins have been locked up in spot ETFs and custodians. ETF shares change hands without any on-chain movement, so realized cap (cost) may not update even when real trading happens. It is a structural change in which on-chain cost becomes increasingly stale relative to the real market's cost, and it muddies direct comparison with past cycles.
  • Realized cap can be distorted by internal exchange transfers. When an exchange reshuffles its cold wallets and moves coins, the "cost" of that supply is re-stamped at the price on the day of the move, even though no real buying occurred. Conversely, coins that change hands inside an exchange leave no on-chain trace, so their cost is not updated.
  • The problem of coins asleep forever. Coins mined early and never moved since (Satoshi's stash and others, estimated in the millions) are counted with a cost near 0. This biases realized cap structurally to the low side.
  • The nature of a full-history σ. σ(market cap), the denominator of MVRV Z, is computed over the full history, so as the market grows σ grows too, and the z amplitude of recent cycles is squeezed compared with the past. It means "extreme values like z > 7 may never appear again," and it is why the thresholds must not be used as absolute standards.

How the broodev bottom score (BOTTOM RADAR) is built — weights disclosed

The BOTTOM RADAR on the broodev dashboard is a separate composite score that answers only one question — not "will it go up if I buy now?" but "are we in a historical bottom zone right now?" Frequently asked questions

Is it always the bottom when MVRV drops below 1?
No. Historically, MVRV < 1 stretches have coincided with major bottoms, but even within those stretches price has fallen a further 20–40% (2011, 2015). Read it not as a "lowest-point signal" but as "a zone for accumulating in stages that historically paid off well 1–2 years later," and remember the sample is only four cycles.
Where can I see realized cap (the average cost basis)?
The charts on this page derive realized cap directly from market cap and MVRV in the CoinMetrics Community API (free, no key required) and draw it. The MVRV·Z card and the bottom score (BOTTOM RADAR) on the broodev dashboard use the same data and the same formulas.
Can I use this method on altcoins too?
Only partially. broodev calculates MVRV Z for the 10 coins for which CoinMetrics provides MVRV (BTC·ETH·XRP·DOGE·BCH·LINK·XLM·LTC·DOT·MANA), and for unsupported coins marks it N/A and renormalizes the weights. Thermocap (cumulative mining rewards) is Bitcoin-only.
Why is it a bottom when market cap falls below the average cost basis?
Market cap below realized cap means holders as a whole are, on average, at a loss. In this zone, capitulation by those who cannot bear their losses peaks, and as that supply passes to long-term holders, selling pressure is exhausted. That said, this is an observation from four past cycles, not a guaranteed law.
