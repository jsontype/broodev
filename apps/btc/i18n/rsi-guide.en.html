When RSI Got It Wrong

Post-mortem report · Last updated: 2026-10-10 · By Y-Systems · Subject: RSI(14, Wilder) and the RSI mapping in broodev Buy Timing Score v1

Model version note — The indicator count (6), weights and worked examples in this article come from the v1 model, before the two on-chain indicators (MVRV Z and Thermocap Z) were added. The dashboard score now uses v2, with 8 indicators and different weights; see the scoring methodology for the current figures.

This is not a how-to guide for RSI. It is a record of the times RSI went badly wrong.
“RSI above 70 means overbought, so sell; below 30 means oversold, so buy” — that one line has been copied across the internet tens of thousands of times, and anyone who traded crypto by that line missed most of every bull market and kept getting trapped in every bear market. We take it apart phase by phase, and at the end we open up how our model handles RSI, and where it still gets things wrong — internal numbers included.

What this article does not cover — The definition and basic formula of RSI are summarized in one paragraph in the Indicator guide, and the weighting scheme for all six indicators is in Scoring methodology. Here we deal only with failures. If RSI is new to you, read those two articles first.

CASE 01 — The market that won’t come down from 70 Critical

RSI’s most expensive failures happen not in bear markets but in bull markets. Because they arrive as opportunity cost rather than losses, many people don’t even count them as failures.

Think back to Q4 2020. Bitcoin started near $10,000 and reached the $50,000s by Q1 2021. Throughout that stretch, the daily RSI stayed pinned above 70 for weeks at a time. Anyone following the textbook sold on the first day RSI crossed 70. Then they watched price go three, four times higher from that very spot. November–December 2017 was exactly the same. After RSI crossed 70, bitcoin went from the $6,000s to nearly $20,000.

What comes next is worse. People who sold at RSI 70 usually never buy back in. They wait for RSI to drop below 30 again, but in a strong uptrend RSI rarely even drops below 40. Pullbacks in an uptrend end not at RSI 30 but around RSI 45–50. In the end they either chase back in at a much higher price or sit out the entire cycle.

Why this happens structurally

RSI is not a measure of “expensive.” What RSI measures is how much larger the average gain over the last 14 days (divided over the 14-day period) is than the average loss on down days. In other words, not the price level but the “consistency” of the rise.

And the very definition of a strong uptrend is “many up days, and down days that don’t fall much.” So RSI 70+ is a symptom of an uptrend, not an anomaly. Just as a fever is a symptom of infection. Taking a fever reducer doesn’t cure the infection, and selling at RSI 70 doesn’t end the rally.

Let’s check the math. Plug in a perfectly ordinary bullish stretch of 10 up days (average +2%) and 4 down days (average −1%) out of 14, and you get average gain = 10×2/14 = 1.43, average loss = 4×1/14 = 0.29, RS = 5.0 → RSI ≈ 83. In terms of return, that’s roughly 16% over two weeks — by crypto standards, not even a violent move. Yet RSI already prints “extremely overbought.” RSI 83 is not an anomaly; it is the arithmetic result of a normal bull market.

■ Autopsy findings

“Overbought = sell” is a rule that holds only in sideways markets, wrongly applied to trending markets. A break above RSI 70 is closer to a trend-confirmation signal than a sell signal. That is why our model’s short-term (momentum) tab uses RSI in its natural direction instead of inverting it — the higher the RSI, the higher the score. We publish the exact function, code and all, in the Verdict section below.

CASE 02 — The market that won’t climb back above 30 Critical

This is the mirror image of CASE 01. And here what disappears is not opportunity cost but real principal.

Throughout 2018, bitcoin fell from near $20,000 to the $3,000s. During that decline RSI dropped below 30 again and again, and every time a flood of “historically oversold” posts followed. Those who bought on that signal saw RSI 30 again in the next leg down. 2022 was a carbon copy. The Luna collapse in May, a further crash in June, the FTX bankruptcy in November — RSI printed oversold in every phase, and every time there was a “lower oversold” still to come.

Here is the key point. RSI’s floor is not 30. It’s 0. Someone who buys at RSI 28 can see RSI 22, and someone who buys at RSI 22 can see RSI 14. And while RSI goes from 30 to 20, price falls another 30–40%.

The illusion created by the word “oversold”

The word oversold is itself the trap. It carries a sense of completion — “it has been sold as much as it can be.” But what RSI 25 actually says is only a past-tense statement: “over the last 14 days, down days overwhelmingly outnumbered up days.” About the future it says not a single word.

Reading it the other way around is actually statistically safer. RSI 25 is a confirmation signal that “a strong downtrend is in progress.” Buying in a downtrend is less “buying something cheap” and more “walking up a down escalator.”

There are rare cases where an RSI extreme was right — and that is the trap. In the COVID crash of 12 March 2020 (−37% in a day), the daily RSI collapsed into the teens, and price rebounded within days. Cases like this stick in memory. Because the hits become news while the misses quietly disappear (survivorship bias). The records of the countless people who bought below RSI 30 in 2018 and 2022 and got trapped are preserved nowhere. Don’t estimate RSI’s win rate from memory. Memory is already biased.

What makes the difference is not the RSI value but the nature of the decline. March 2020 was a liquidity shock — the kind of event that ends within days. 2018 and 2022 were cycle retracements — the kind of event that lasts more than a year. RSI cannot tell these two apart. Both output exactly the same “RSI 20.” To tell them apart you need information from outside RSI — valuation (the Mayer Multiple), cycle drawdown, a trend filter. That is the only reason to combine indicators at all.

■ Autopsy findings

RSI 30 is not a buy signal but an “entering the zone of interest” signal — and only when other indicators agree. That is why our model’s long-term (contrarian) tab inverts RSI yet lowers its weight to 18% instead of 28%. From a contrarian standpoint RSI is merely supporting evidence; the main evidence is the Mayer Multiple and the 365-day drawdown.

CASE 03 — The two faces of divergence Undetermined

Once RSI’s failures became widely known, the remedy that appeared was divergence. Price makes a new low, but RSI is higher than at the previous low → bullish divergence → a rebound is imminent. The opposite is bearish divergence. This is an attempt to fix RSI’s failures with RSI. Which is why it only half works.

What divergence actually says (the part most articles skip)

Stated precisely, the mathematical meaning of a bullish divergence is this. 

Price: low A ＞ low B   (lower low)
RSI  : low A ＜ low B   (higher RSI)

→ Translation: "Price fell further. But it fell less violently."

That’s all. It is not the statement “this is the bottom.” It is the statement “the pace of decline has slowed.” And the pace of decline doesn’t slow only at bottoms. Merely shifting from a crash into a slow, dragging decline is enough to generate a divergence automatically. That’s what the bullish divergence of May–June 2022 was. The pace of decline clearly slowed, and afterwards price fell nearly another 40%.

Bearish divergence is no different. The bearish divergence just before the $64,000 top in April 2021 was right — price halved a month later. But throughout the Q4 2020 rally, bearish divergences appeared repeatedly and were repeatedly invalidated. Same pattern, opposite outcomes. In hindsight you can tell them apart; in advance there is no way to.

Why we don’t publish a “divergence win rate of ○○%”

RSI articles on the internet often attach numbers like “RSI divergence win rate: 70%.” We will not publish such a number. The honest reason is that it cannot be calculated.

  • The definition of a “low” is arbitrary. Depending on whether you set the minimum spacing between two lows at 5 days or 20 days, the number of divergences changes several-fold.
  • The definition of “success” is arbitrary. How many % within how many days counts as success? 2% within 3 days is a success, anything else a failure? Shift the criteria just a little and the win rate moves from the 40s to the 70s.
  • Whoever picks the parameters picks the result. If you keep changing the combination until you find one that yields a 70% win rate, anyone can produce a 70% win rate. That is not a discovery but overfitting.

That is why our model’s code contains no divergence logic at all. We compute the RSI time series (rsiWilder(closes, 14)), but what goes into the scoring function is only the last value. The rest of the series is used only for the sparkline chart on screen. It contributes not even 1% to the score.

Rules that cannot be reproduced do not go into the score — that is our principle. Divergence is “the discretion of the person reading the chart,” not a “function.” The moment discretion enters, the score becomes a different value every time, and then there is no point in publishing it. This doesn’t mean divergence is useless. It means we can’t turn it into an honest number.
■ Autopsy findings

If you insist on using divergence, at least use it only together with a trend filter. A bullish divergence + the 50-day above the 200-day (golden state) + the MACD histogram turning up — when those three line up, it is an entirely different event from a divergence in the middle of a death cross. The latter appeared countless times in 2022 and mostly failed.

CASE 04 — A phantom signal created by the formula Technical

So far the failures came from misreading RSI. This one is different. It is a case where the signal flips entirely just because of how RSI was calculated. And most investors don’t even know it exists.

In practice there are two families of RSI.

  • Wilder RSI — the original by its creator, J. Welles Wilder. It updates the average as (previous average × 13 + today’s value) ÷ 14. Because it is exponential smoothing, the past lingers forever, a little at a time.
  • Cutler RSI (simple-average RSI) — takes a plain arithmetic mean of the last 14 days. Data from 15 days ago falls completely out of the window.

Many charting tools just label it “RSI” without saying which one. But the difference in results is anything but trivial.

Walkthrough: how RSI jumps from 37 to 96 in a single day

This is a constructed example. Suppose daily price changes are as follows (units are arbitrary).

Day 1 : −20    ← crash (e.g. a −25% plunge in one day)
Days 2–15 : +1 every day    (except day 8: −0.5)

In other words, after the one-day crash, nothing changes through day 15.
Price is just quietly recovering by the same amount every day.

Now compute RSI for day 14 and day 15 both ways.

PointWilder RSICutler (simple-average) RSI
Day 1436.936.9 (identical — both seed the first value with a simple average)
Day 1539.0 (+2.1)96.3 (+59.4)

The only thing price did on day 15 was “+1.” Exactly the same behavior as day 14. Yet the simple-average RSI leapt from 36.9 to 96.3 — more than 59 points in a single day.

The reason is simple. The moment day 15 arrives, day 1’s −20 crash drops out of the 14-day window. With the crash gone entirely from the average-loss calculation, the denominator collapsed and RSI shot to the ceiling. This is called the drop-off effect. The market did nothing. The calendar did it.

How much this phantom shakes our score

Here is what you get when you feed both values into our model’s actual RSI conversion function. (The full function is in the Verdict section below.)

Had we used a simple-average RSI, the short-term composite score would have been inflated by nearly 19 points while the long-term composite score was cut by more than 12. On a 100-point scale, 19 points means jumping an entire band, from NEUTRAL (50) to ACCUMULATE/BUY (69). A buy signal switches on while price has done nothing at all.

That is why we use only the Wilder method. In Wilder, a crash’s impact doesn’t “drop out” of the window; it fades gradually. Each day that passes, the shock shrinks by a factor of 13/14 ≈ 0.9286. Working it out:

Time since crashShare of the shock remaining in the Wilder average

The shock half-life of Wilder RSI is about 9.4 days. This matters because it means a major crash still leaves roughly a 10% trace in RSI a month later. That may look like a flaw, but it actually more closely resembles how markets really remember. The month after a big crash is still inside “the crash’s shadow,” and we want that trace to remain in the score.

■ Autopsy findings

Always check whether the RSI in your charting tool is Wilder or simple-average. Same coin, same 14 days, same date — RSI 39 and RSI 96 can exist at the same time. And one of them is a phantom made purely by the calendar.

Verdict — why we give RSI 28% short-term and 18% long-term

The findings of the four autopsies above are the design rationale for our model. Here is the code, verbatim. 

// Short-term (momentum) — the higher the RSI, the higher the score (natural direction)
momRsi = clamp(50 + (RSI − 50) × 1.6, 0, 100)

// Long-term (contrarian) — the lower the RSI, the higher the score (inverted)
conRsi = clamp(50 + (50 − RSI) × 1.7, 0, 100)

The signs are opposite. The same RSI value produces opposite scores in the two tabs. A table makes it clear.

The remedy for CASE 01 is momRsi. In the short-term tab, RSI 80 is not “sell” but 27.4 points of evidence to buy. We blocked the “overbought, so sell” formula for ruin at the code level. The remedy for CASE 02 is conRsi. In the long-term tab, RSI 25 becomes 16.7 points of evidence to buy. But since it isn’t enough on its own, we pressed its weight down to 18%.

What “28% vs 18%” really means — a question of direction, not quality

Many people read these numbers as “RSI is more accurate in the short term.” That’s a misreading. The correct reading is this.

  • In the short term (1–3 months), RSI can be used in its natural direction, and used that way it works well. Over short stretches bitcoin behaves like a momentum asset, and RSI is the most direct measure of momentum. Since there is a direction in which it’s usable, we give it a large weight (28%).
  • In the long term (1 year+), RSI’s window (14 days) is far too short. It’s like trying to measure a four-year cycle with a 14-day ruler. The tool doesn’t fit the object. So the main evidence for cycle judgment comes from the Mayer Multiple (based on the 200-day average, 18%) and the 365-day drawdown (14%), and RSI gets just 18% in the role of a proxy for capitulation. We didn’t set it to 0% because it is still valid in extreme zones (20 and below).

What the weight table hides — RSI’s real dominance is greater than 28%

Here we disclose something we ourselves realized only belatedly. Looking only at the weight percentages makes you under- or overestimate each indicator’s influence. What matters is weight × the range that indicator actually moves through.

Short-term tabNominal weightRealistic sub-score rangeEffective swing in composite score
RSI28%RSI 15–90 → 0 ~ 100 (full range used)28.0 pts
Mayer Multiple4%Multiple 0.5–2.5 → 37.5 ~ 87.5 (only half used)2.0 pts

By weight alone, RSI is 7 times Mayer (28÷4). But once you factor in the actual swing, it’s 14 times. The 1.6 slope of momRsi amplifies RSI across the full 0–100 range, whereas the slope of 25 in momMayer can use only half of the sub-score within the realistic multiple range (0.5–2.5).

This is not good news. The short-term composite score depends on RSI more heavily than we intended. And RSI, as we saw in CASE 01 and 02, is precisely the indicator that is structurally wrong in trending markets. In other words, our short-term model is most heavily exposed to its weakest indicator. We keep digging into this problem in the next section.

Confession — where our model still gets things wrong because of RSI

Articles that sell indicators praise indicators. We’ll do the opposite. Below are flaws that actually exist in our code. Some can be fixed; some cannot.

  1. Saturation zones — we cannot tell RSI 82 from RSI 95.
    momRsi gives 100 points to everything at RSI 81.25 and above. conRsi gives 100 points to everything at RSI 20.59 and below. So the long-term model sees the March 2020 COVID crash (RSI in the teens) and an ordinary correction (RSI 20) as the same value. It means zero resolution in the extreme zones that matter most. This is the price of clamping, and we haven’t found a better solution yet.
  2. The blow-off top guard depends on the Mayer Multiple — and without it, the guard doesn’t trigger.
    The short-term tab has a safety mechanism against buying the cycle top: if (mayer > 2.0) score × (1 − (mayer − 2.0) × 0.55). Why is it needed? Because without the guard, our short-term model prints STRONG BUY at the top of a bubble. Worked out by hand: 
    Assumption — cycle top: RSI 88 · Fear & Greed 94 · Mayer 2.5 · Drawdown −2% · golden state

RSI       sub-score 100 (saturated) × 28% = 28.0   ← the biggest block
F&G       sub-score 100 (saturated) × 28% = 28.0
Drawdown  sub-score  97.4           × 18% = 17.5
MACD      sub-score  90             × 14% = 12.6
Cross     sub-score  65             ×  8% =  5.2
Mayer     sub-score  87.5           ×  4% =  3.5
─────────────────────────────────────
Raw score = 94.8  →  STRONG BUY (at the bubble top!)

Applying the blow-off guard:
  Mayer 2.5 → × 0.725  →  68.8
  F&G 94 ≥ 90 → − 8     →  60.8  →  NEUTRAL
    The guard shaved off 34 points. But look at the code, and the condition is mayer != null && mayer > 2.0. For a newly listed asset without 200 days of data, the Mayer Multiple is null, so this guard never runs at all. If such an asset goes on a parabolic rally, our short-term score can print STRONG BUY at the top. And the biggest block of that score is RSI’s 28 points. This is a known flaw, and the dashboard’s coverage indicator (coverage 5/6) is the only warning mechanism.
  3. When the two tabs point in opposite directions, we don’t know which one is right.
    Feed in a capitulation phase with RSI 14, Fear & Greed 6, Mayer 0.52 and drawdown −74%, and you get this.
    Short-term (momentum)Long-term (contrarian)
    RSI sub-score0.0100.0
    RSI contribution0.0 pts18.0 pts
    Composite score11.7 → AVOID83.5 → STRONG BUY
    Same moment, same data, a 71.8-point difference. It’s not a bug but the design — the time horizons are different. But honestly, this model cannot tell you “which tab is right for you right now.” That depends on your holding period and risk tolerance, and our score cannot decide it for you.
  4. The 14-day period is just convention.
    It’s the number Wilder chose in 1978 while looking at stock and commodity markets. It is not a value validated for crypto, which trades 24 hours a day. The optimal window almost certainly differs by coin and by volatility regime. Even so, we fixed it at 14. Tuning it per coin would make performance look better, but that is just overfitting, and it falls apart in the future. It’s accurate to call it “a value we fixed knowing it’s second-best.”
  5. The decimal places mean nothing.
    RSI varies depending on which exchange’s closing price, at what time, you use. UTC midnight and KST (Korea time) midnight produce different values. With different sources, a difference of 2–3 in the same day’s RSI is common. That then rides the 1.6 slope of momRsi and automatically puts an error of about 0.9 points into the composite score. The first decimal place of our score is noise. Don’t try to distinguish 68.4 from 68.9.
  6. RSI creates no new information.
    This may be the most important sentence in this article. RSI is a value derived from price. It merely rearranges and redisplays what price has already told you; it adds no information that wasn’t already in the market. RSI is a lagging indicator, and lagging indicators don’t know the future. This is not a fixable flaw but the nature of the indicator.

So should we throw RSI away? — No, use it like this

We’ve been through four autopsies and six confessions, but the conclusion isn’t “RSI is garbage.” It’s “RSI is not a standalone signal.” Condensed into practical rules:

  1. Read the trend before you read RSI. First check whether the 50-day is above or below the 200-day. Without knowing the trend state, an RSI value cannot be interpreted.
  2. In an uptrend, RSI’s floor is 40–50, not 30. RSI 45 in this regime isn’t “oversold” but a pullback. Conversely, in a downtrend, RSI’s ceiling is 50–60, not 70. In a bear market, RSI 60 may be not “overbought” but the end of a relief bounce. The 30/70 numbers are for sideways markets only.
  3. Between 30 and 70, RSI says almost nothing. Don’t make trading decisions on RSI moves within this range. RSI only carries meaning on its own at extremes (20 and below / 85 and above), and even there it needs other indicators to agree.
  4. Check that your tool’s RSI is Wilder. As we saw in CASE 04, a simple-average RSI produces phantom signals made by the calendar.
  5. Don’t be surprised when the dashboard’s two tabs disagree. That is useful information: “a phase where the short term and long term point in opposite directions.” It usually shows up at bottoms (long-term buy / short-term avoid) or tops (short-term buy / long-term avoid).
  6. Check coverage. A display like coverage 5/6 (82% weight) means one indicator is missing, and if the missing one is the Mayer Multiple, it means the blow-off guard is off (Confession no. 2).
This article in one line
RSI measures not “how expensive” but “how consistently it has risen lately.” The moment you confuse the two — that is, the moment you translate it as “overbought = expensive = sell” — RSI becomes a loss machine.
