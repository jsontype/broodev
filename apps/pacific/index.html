What is Pacific Panel?

Pacific Panel is a free, data-light utility page for daily life in Fiji, Samoa, Tonga and Vanuatu. It puts four essentials on one fast page: a cyclone dashboard — season status, the category 1–5 scale explained for atolls versus high islands, and one-tap links to RSMC Nadi, the Samoa, Tonga and Vanuatu met services and Pacific tsunami warnings; an ocean dashboard with live wave height, wave period and tide times for each capital; money tools — live AUD, NZD and USD rates against FJD, WST, TOP and VUV plus a remittance true-cost calculator; and honest, plain-English guides to the Australia PALM scheme and New Zealand RSE seasonal-work programmes. No app install, no account, no ads — it loads fast on island mobile data and old phones.

The Australia PALM scheme, explained

The Pacific Australia Labour Mobility (PALM) scheme lets workers from nine Pacific countries and Timor-Leste take up jobs in rural and regional Australia where local workers are scarce — mainly agriculture, meat processing, hospitality and aged care. There are two streams: short-term placements of up to nine months (seasonal harvest-type work) and long-term placements of one to four years in the same job. Only Australian businesses vetted and approved as PALM employers can hire under the scheme, and they must meet minimum-hour guarantees, pay Australian wages and help arrange accommodation and welfare support. Workers are recruited through their own government's labour sending unit, not by private brokers. Citizens of Fiji, Samoa, Tonga and Vanuatu are all eligible to register — see the eligibility notes below.

The New Zealand RSE scheme, explained

New Zealand's Recognised Seasonal Employer (RSE) scheme brings Pacific workers to plant, maintain, harvest and pack crops in horticulture and viticulture. Workers can stay up to seven months in any eleven-month period (nine months for citizens of Kiribati and Tuvalu because of travel distance). Only employers holding current RSE status may recruit, and they must pay at least the market rate, provide pastoral care and subsidise half the return airfare. Fiji, Samoa, Tonga and Vanuatu are among the participating states, and Vanuatu has historically been the single largest sender of RSE workers. Demand routinely exceeds places, so registration through your government's official work-ready pool — not through individuals promising shortcuts — is the only path that counts.

How recruitment really works — and the red flags

Both schemes share one golden rule: workers never pay to be recruited. Legitimate recruitment flows through your country's labour sending unit (Fiji's National Employment Centre, Samoa's Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Labour, Tonga's Ministry of Internal Affairs, Vanuatu's Department of Labour) and approved employers or licensed agents they recognise. Watch for these red flags:

  • Anyone asking for a "registration fee", "visa fee" or "guarantee payment" to get you on a list — scam.
  • Job offers over Facebook or WhatsApp from people you have never met, especially with pressure to pay quickly.
  • "Agents" who cannot name the approved employer, the farm or the region you would work in.
  • Requests to hand over your passport to a third party for "processing".
  • Promises of guaranteed selection — no legitimate actor can promise that.

If in doubt, verify the employer on palmscheme.gov.au (approved employer list) or check RSE employer status with Immigration New Zealand before signing anything.

Why Pacific remittances are the world's most expensive

Money sent home from Australia and New Zealand is a lifeline — for Tonga and Samoa, remittances are worth roughly a third and a quarter of GDP respectively. Yet the Pacific is consistently the world's most expensive remittance corridor: sending money to the islands has averaged around 8–10 per cent of the amount sent, far above the global average (~6%) and more than triple the UN Sustainable Development Goal target of 3 per cent. The causes stack up: tiny transfer volumes, few competing providers, cash-heavy pickup networks, and banks "de-risking" — closing the accounts of small money-transfer operators — which thins competition further. On a typical NZ$200 transfer, fees and margin can quietly consume NZ$20: two full days of RSE wages gone before the money lands.

How to compare the true cost of a transfer

The advertised fee is only half the story — the other half hides in the exchange-rate margin. A provider advertising "zero fees" while offering a rate 4 per cent below mid-market is more expensive than one charging a flat $5 at the mid-market rate. To compare properly: take the mid-market rate (what this page shows), multiply your send amount by it, and compare that with what the recipient actually gets. The difference, expressed as a percentage, is your true cost. The MONEY tab's calculator does this arithmetic for you, and SendMoneyPacific — a free comparison site funded by the Australian and New Zealand governments — publishes side-by-side provider costs for every major Pacific corridor. Checking it before each transfer routinely saves several per cent.

Cyclone categories: atolls versus high islands

The South-West Pacific uses the Australian tropical-cyclone intensity scale, and RSMC Nadi (Fiji Meteorological Service) issues the official regional warnings. What each category means on the ground depends heavily on the island under it. On high volcanic islands (Viti Levu, Upolu, Efate) the main killers are wind damage, landslides and flash-flooding rivers. On low atolls and coastal flats the deadliest hazard is storm surge and wave over-wash — a category 3 system can push the ocean across an entire islet only a few metres above sea level, contaminating groundwater and stripping crops even where buildings survive the wind. That is why atoll evacuation decisions are made earlier and at lower categories than wind-only guidance would suggest.

Cyclone season: a preparation timeline

The official South-West Pacific cyclone season runs 1 November to 30 April, peaking January–March; out-of-season systems happen but are rare. A sensible household timeline:

King tides and coastal flooding, explained

"King tide" is the popular name for the highest astronomical tides of the year, which occur when a new or full moon coincides with the moon's closest approach to Earth. In the Pacific they typically cluster around December–February and June–July. A king tide on a calm day may just brim the seawall — but the same tide plus a distant swell, La Niña sea-level anomalies, or an onshore blow can flood roads, taro pits and homes with the ocean doing nothing "stormy" at all. Because mean sea level around the western Pacific is rising at roughly two to three times the global average rate, yesterday's rare nuisance flood is becoming an annual event. The OCEAN tab's tide chart helps you see the next high water; local met services publish king-tide calendars each season.

Tsunami awareness: the other ocean hazard

Fiji, Samoa, Tonga and Vanuatu all sit on or near active plate boundaries, and Pacific tsunami history is recent, not theoretical: the 2009 South Pacific tsunami struck Samoa and northern Tonga within about fifteen minutes of the earthquake, and the 2022 Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai eruption sent waves across Tongatapu with almost no lead time. The lesson is that for local-source events, the ground itself is the warning: if shaking is strong or lasts long, if the sea suddenly withdraws, or if you hear an unusual roar, move inland or uphill immediately — do not wait for a siren or a message. Official threat messages from the Pacific Tsunami Warning Center and your national geohazards office (VMGD in Vanuatu handles earthquakes, volcanoes and tsunami together) matter most for distant-source events, where you may have hours of warning.

Frequently asked questions

When is cyclone season in the South Pacific?

Officially 1 November to 30 April, with the peak from January to March. Fiji, Samoa, Tonga and Vanuatu all sit inside the main South-West Pacific cyclone belt; out-of-season cyclones are possible but rare.

Do I have to pay an agent to join PALM or RSE?

No — never. Recruitment runs through your government's labour sending unit and approved employers. Anyone demanding a payment to "register" you, "secure" a visa or "guarantee" selection is running a scam and should be reported.

How long can I work in Australia or New Zealand?

PALM short-term placements run up to 9 months and long-term placements 1–4 years. RSE allows up to 7 months in any 11-month period (9 months for Kiribati and Tuvalu citizens).

Why does my family receive less than the rate I see online?

Providers add a margin to the exchange rate on top of any fee. Compare the mid-market rate shown here with the provider's rate, and check SendMoneyPacific for the cheapest current option on your corridor — differences of several per cent are common.

Are the tide times on this page official?

No — they are model estimates from the Open-Meteo marine API, good for spotting the rhythm of high and low water. For navigation, anchoring or reef crossings, use your national hydrographic or met service tide tables.

What is a king tide?

The highest astronomical tides of the year, when a new or full moon lines up with the moon's closest approach to Earth. Combined with swell or high sea-level anomalies they can flood low coasts without any storm nearby.

What should I do if I feel a strong earthquake near the coast?

Move inland or to high ground immediately — strong or long shaking, a sudden sea withdrawal or an unusual roar are natural tsunami warnings. For local events the wave can arrive in minutes, before any official message.

Which currencies does the converter support?

The panel focuses on the four national currencies — Fiji dollar (FJD), Samoan tālā (WST), Tongan pa'anga (TOP) and Vanuatu vatu (VUV) — against AUD, NZD and USD, using daily mid-market reference rates. The terminal's convert command accepts any ISO currency code the rate feed carries.

Does this page work offline or on slow connections?

The page itself is a single small HTML file with no frameworks, fonts or ad scripts, so it loads quickly on 3G. Live data (rates, waves, wind) needs a connection, but the last fetched exchange rates are cached in your browser and reused if the network drops.
